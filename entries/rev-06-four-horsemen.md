## THE READING

### 1 · Faithful translation

*A faithful translation made directly from the Greek, kept close to the wording so the choices that matter stay visible. The hard spots — the colours, the coins, the names — are opened up in the next section.*

1 Then I watched as the Lamb opened the first of the seven seals, and I heard one of the four living creatures say, in a voice like thunder, "Come!" 2 And I looked, and there was a white horse. Its rider held a bow, and a victor's wreath was given to him, and he rode out conquering, and to conquer.

3 When the Lamb opened the second seal, I heard the second living creature say, "Come!" 4 And out came another horse, fiery red. Its rider was allowed to take peace from the earth, so that people would kill one another, and he was given a great sword.

5 When the Lamb opened the third seal, I heard the third living creature say, "Come!" And I looked, and there was a black horse. Its rider held a balance in his hand. 6 And I heard what sounded like a voice from among the four living creatures, saying, "A quart of wheat for a denarius, and three quarts of barley for a denarius — but do not harm the oil and the wine."

7 When the Lamb opened the fourth seal, I heard the voice of the fourth living creature say, "Come!" 8 And I looked, and there was a sickly pale horse. Its rider was named Death, and Hades came close behind him. They were given authority over a quarter of the earth, to kill by sword and famine and plague, and by the wild beasts of the earth.

### 2 · How the translations differ — and what's at stake

These eight verses are vivid and compressed, and a handful of small choices — a colour, a coin, a name — quietly decide how the scene reads. The public-domain English versions you are likeliest to meet (the King James Version of 1611, the American Standard Version of 1901, the modern World English Bible) part ways at several of them, and the underlying Greek hides distinctions that plain English flattens. Five places carry most of the weight.

- **6:1 — "Come!" or "Come and see"?** The critical Greek text has each living creature say a single word, *erchou*, "Come!" — a summons that calls the next horseman onto the stage. Later manuscripts, which the King James follows, add two words: "Come *and see*," turning the line into an invitation to John to watch. The difference changes who is in charge of the scene. On the older reading the four creatures around God's throne are *unleashing* the riders, one by one, at the Lamb's command; on the King James reading they are merely ushering the seer to the window. The riders ride because heaven calls them.

- **6:2 — a wreath, not a crown.** The first rider is given a *stephanos* — the woven garland awarded to a victor or an athlete — not a *diadēma*, the jewelled band of a king. English versions that simply say "a crown" lose the distinction, and it matters, because the same Greek author later crowns the returning Christ of Revelation 19 with "many *diadems*." The word-choice is one of the few hard clues in the long argument over who this first rider is (a question taken up below, under *Interpretation*): a victor's wreath fits a conqueror — divine, human, or demonic — better than it fits a reigning king.

- **6:6 — "a measure of wheat for a penny" hides a famine.** The King James "a measure of wheat for a penny" sounds cheap; the Greek says the opposite. A *choinix* of wheat — about one person's grain for a day — costs a *denarius*, which was a labourer's wage for a day. A full day's pay for a single day's bread is famine pricing: you work all day and eat, but you do not feed a family, and there is nothing left over. Meanwhile the voice spares "the oil and the wine" — the staples of the comfortable. The "penny" is the single most misleading word in the passage; the line is about hunger and inequality, not a bargain.

- **6:8 — "pale," and "Hell."** The fourth horse is *chlōros* — the word for the green of new grass, used here for the greenish pallor of a corpse. "Pale" undersells it; the horse is the colour of death itself. And the King James says "*Hell* followed with him," where the Greek is *Hadēs* — the shadowy realm of the dead, the place everyone goes, not the fiery hell of later punishment. "Hell" smuggles in a doctrine the word does not carry; *Death* and *the Grave* riding together is closer.

- **6:8 — the fourth weapon is plague, and it is a quotation.** The rider kills "by sword and famine and *death* and the wild beasts." That third item, Greek *thanatos*, "death," is the word the old Greek Old Testament regularly used to render the Hebrew *deber*, "pestilence." Read that way the list becomes sword, famine, *plague*, and wild beasts — and those are, exactly and in order, the "four deadly judgments" God names in **Ezekiel 14:21**. Translations that keep a flat "death" here hide both the plague and the fact that John is quoting scripture. The horseman is not inventing the catalogue of ruin; he is enacting an old one.

### 3 · Plain-English retelling

In the vision just before this one, John has seen a sealed scroll in the hand of God, and only one figure is found worthy to open it: a Lamb that looks as if it has been slaughtered and yet is standing — the book's image for the crucified and risen Christ. *(A "seal" was a blob of wax pressed over a rolled-up document; breaking it was how you opened and enacted what was inside. This scroll has seven. Opening them, one by one, is what sets the rest of Revelation in motion.)* Now the Lamb begins to break them.

The first seal snaps. One of the four strange winged creatures that stand around God's throne calls out, in a voice like thunder: *Come.* And a white horse rides out. Its rider carries a bow and is handed a winner's wreath, and he goes out "conquering, and to conquer." *(Who he is has been argued about for two thousand years — the triumphant gospel, the Antichrist, or simply Conquest itself. The text refuses to say, and that silence is part of the design.)*

The Lamb breaks the second seal. *Come.* Out comes a horse the colour of fire. Its rider is given the power to take peace away from the world, so that people fall on each other, and he is handed a great sword. This is War — not a battle, but the unbinding of slaughter, neighbour against neighbour.

The third seal. *Come.* A black horse, and its rider holds a set of merchant's scales. A voice from among the creatures calls out prices: *a whole day's wage for one day's bread; the same for a little barley — but don't touch the oil and the wine.* *(In plain terms: grain has become so scarce that an ordinary worker spends everything just to eat, while the luxuries of the rich are left untouched. This is Famine, and the kind that falls hardest on the poor.)*

The fourth seal. *Come.* And the worst of them rides out: a horse the sick green of a corpse. Its rider has a name — **Death** — and right behind him rides **Hades**, the grave itself, swallowing what Death cuts down. *(Hades here is the land of the dead, where everyone was thought to go, not a place of fiery torment.)* The two of them are handed authority over a quarter of the earth, to kill four ways: by the sword, by hunger, by plague, and by wild animals. *(Those four are lifted straight out of the prophet Ezekiel — the standard list of how a civilization dies.)*

Four riders, and the catastrophes are the oldest ones there are: conquest, war, famine, death. The point the vision keeps making is the quiet one easy to miss — it is the *Lamb* who opens each seal, and the *creatures of heaven* who summon each rider. Even these horrors do not ride on their own.

### 4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)

The Four Horsemen are usually pictured as the world breaking loose, but the frame of the scene says almost the opposite. Every rider comes out only when the slain Lamb breaks a seal and a creature at God's own throne says "Come." Conquest, war, famine, and death — the four engines of human misery — are shown here not as chaos that has escaped, but as forces under authority, let out one at a time and held to a limit: their reach is "a quarter of the earth," not all of it. The vision's grim comfort, such as it is, lies entirely in that framing. History's terrors are real and are not explained away, but they are not the last word, and they are not in charge.

It also reads as a brutally honest catalogue of how the world actually goes wrong, and in a revealing order. Conquest opens the door; war follows; war breeds famine; famine and its diseases end in death. The third rider's price-list is the sharpest touch — a day's wage for a day's bread while the rich keep their wine — because it says the suffering is not evenly shared. This is what apocalyptic writing is for: not a coded calendar of the future, but a way of seeing the present clearly, with the veil pulled back, so that the ordinary catastrophes of history are shown for what they are.

And the first rider keeps the whole thing unsettled. By refusing to say whether the white horse is salvation or menace, the text makes the reader decide what they are looking at — which is exactly the demand apocalyptic literature likes to make.

### 5 · Why this matters

This is the source of one of the most portable images in the language. **"The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse"** is shorthand, instantly understood, for any set of looming, world-ending threats — and the **"four horsemen of X"** construction has spread far past religion. The New Atheist writers Dawkins, Dennett, Harris, and Hitchens were dubbed (and embraced) "the Four Horsemen." The psychologist John Gottman calls criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling the **"four horsemen"** that predict divorce. Sportswriting's most famous sentence, Grantland Rice's 1924 lead, christened a Notre Dame backfield "the Four Horsemen." Whenever someone groups four threats and calls them horsemen, this passage is the well they are drawing from.

The imagery itself is everywhere once you know it. Albrecht Dürer's 1498 woodcut of the four riders trampling humanity is one of the most reproduced images in Western art; the "pale horse" and "pale rider" are stock names for death (Clint Eastwood's *Pale Rider*, the David Lynch line, a hundred metal songs); a 1916 novel and its Valentino film carried the title into popular culture; the X-Men villain Apocalypse rides with his own Four Horsemen. And the apocalyptic vocabulary of the news — "war, famine, plague, and death," the "horsemen" of a crisis — comes straight from these eight verses. Knowing the passage lets you hear how much of our talk about catastrophe is borrowed from a vision John had on a prison island.

## BEHIND THE TEXT

### Setting & Context
**When (in the story):** the opening of the first four of seven seals on the scroll that only the Lamb can open (Revelation 5) — the first movement of the book's great sequence of judgments.
**When (scholarly):** most place Revelation in the mid-90s CE, late in the reign of the emperor Domitian, written to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia (western Turkey) under the pressure of imperial cult and intermittent hostility; a minority argues for the late 60s, around Nero and the Jewish War. Either way it is the work of a real first-century crisis, not a forecast detached from its moment.
**Where:** John says he received the vision on **Patmos**, a small Aegean island, "on account of the word of God" (1:9) — traditionally an exile. The vision's furniture is the Roman world: the dreaded mounted archers of **Parthia** on Rome's eastern frontier (the white horse with its bow), the grain economy and the silver **denarius** (the third seal), the standard miseries of empire.
**Background a modern reader needs:** apocalyptic literature is a genre, not a forecast — highly symbolic visionary writing, born in times of crisis, that pulls back the veil ("apocalypse" means *unveiling*) on the real forces behind history. The scroll, its seven seals, the slain-yet-standing **Lamb**, and the **four living creatures** (the winged throne-attendants of 4:6–8, drawn from Ezekiel and Isaiah) are all established in chapters 4–5 before the seals are opened here.
**Cast:** **The Lamb** — the crucified and risen Christ, the only one worthy to open the scroll, who breaks each seal. **The four living creatures** — throne-attendants who summon the riders. **The four riders** — Conquest (white), War (red), Famine (black), and **Death** (pale), with **Hades**, the realm of the dead, riding behind the fourth.

### Text & Composition
**The words are doing precise work.** *stephanos* (6:2) is a victor's *wreath*, not the royal *diadēma*; *chlōros* (6:8) is sickly green, the colour of a corpse; a *choinix* of grain for a *dēnarion* (6:6) is a day's bread for a day's wage; *machaira* (6:4) is a sword; and the fourth rider is *Thanatos* (Death) trailed by *Hadēs* (the Grave). The fourth seal's *thanatos*-as-pestilence and the whole "sword, famine, plague, beasts" list quote **Ezekiel 14:21**. *(How these choices land in English, and what rides on the "Come" / "Come and see" variant, is opened up for the reader in The Reading → "How the translations differ"; the technical apparatus is not repeated here.)*
**Textual notes & variants:** the main variant is the *erchou* / *erchou kai ide* ("Come" vs. "Come and see") reading at each seal — handled in §2.
**Authorship & date (traditional):** the apostle John, the son of Zebedee, exiled to Patmos, writing in the 90s CE.
**Authorship & date (scholarly):** the author names himself simply "John" and writes a distinctive, Semitically-coloured Greek unlike the Fourth Gospel's; most scholars take him to be a Jewish-Christian prophet known as **John of Patmos**, not necessarily the apostle. The Domitianic date (c. 95) is the majority view; a Neronian date (late 60s) is the main alternative.

### Is it historical?
This is the wrong question for the genre, and almost no reader of any kind asks it of the horsemen as such. **What the text claims:** not that four literal riders crossed a literal plain, but that John *saw* them in a vision — symbolic figures for conquest, war, famine, and death. **What evidence could show:** nothing, because no datable event is being asserted; this is visionary, not chronicle. **What scholars conclude:** the riders are apocalyptic symbols. Many also read them as encoding John's own world — the first horseman as the feared **Parthian** archers, the famine of the third seal as the grain shortages of the Roman East — so the vision *reflects* real history without *recording* an event. Where readers disagree is on time-frame, not facticity (see *Interpretation* and *Literal or figurative?*).
**Grade — as a datable event:** no testable historical claim is made; this is **rejected** as chronicle, which for apocalyptic is not a criticism but a category. **As a window on the real anxieties and economy of the first-century Roman East:** **well-attested.**

### Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted
**The book is read through four lenses, and they shape everything.** *Preterist* readers take Revelation to be about John's own day — Rome, Nero or Domitian, the Parthian threat — so the horsemen are first-century forces. *Historicist* readers (dominant from the Middle Ages through the Reformation) map the seals onto the sweep of church history, the horsemen as successive empires or eras. *Futurist* readers, especially modern dispensationalists, place the seals in a still-future "Tribulation," so the horsemen are coming literal events. *Idealist* (or symbolic) readers — the mainstream of Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant scholarship — take the horsemen as a timeless disclosure of the recurring pattern of conquest, war, famine, and death in every age.
**The first rider is the oldest argument.** Some Fathers — **Irenaeus**, and the earliest commentator on Revelation, **Victorinus of Pettau** — read the white horse as **Christ or the conquering gospel** going out into the world, linking it to the white-horse rider of Revelation 19. Many later and modern readers, noting that the rider stands at the head of war, famine, and death, take him instead as **conquest** or even the **Antichrist** — a counterfeit of Christ. The text's refusal to name him keeps both readings alive.
**Reception in the traditions.** In the Christian East, Revelation was the last book to gain secure canonical standing and is still never read in the Orthodox liturgy, which tempered its influence there. In the West it shaped medieval apocalyptic thought (notably **Joachim of Fiore**'s scheme of historical ages) and, from the 19th century, the dispensationalism of the **Scofield Reference Bible** and popular works like *The Late Great Planet Earth* and the *Left Behind* novels. **Judaism** does not receive Revelation at all, so there is no Jewish reading of this text.

### Allegorical reading (figural & typological senses)
*Revelation is already a symbolic book, so the line between "interpreting" it and "allegorizing" it is thin; the readings below are the explicitly figural moves later readers made on top of the vision, each attributed and graded.*

- **The white rider as Christ / the Word (Christian typology).** Irenaeus and Victorinus read the first horseman as Christ or the gospel "going forth conquering," a figure of the Word's advance through the world, cross-referenced to the white-horse Christ of Revelation 19:11. *Grade:* **partly invited** — the shared white horse and the language of conquering give the text a real handhold — but resisted by readers who note the rider's company (war, famine, death).
- **The fourfold scourge as the moral shape of judgment.** Read tropologically, the four riders become the standard form sin's consequences take — the violence, scarcity, and death that follow when peace is "taken from the earth" — a moral reading of history rather than a prediction. *Grade:* **a moral application**, lightly attested.
- **Joachim of Fiore's historical-figural scheme.** The medieval abbot read the seals as figures of successive ages of the church, a reading that fed centuries of historicist interpretation. *Grade:* a system the reader **builds**, influential but not demanded by the text.

### Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)
*Owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona.* Revelation sits at one extreme of the spectrum, and the split is sharp and consequential. **Read as literal, future events:** dispensationalist and much of conservative evangelical, fundamentalist, and Pentecostal Christianity, which expects a literal end-times Tribulation in which a real global conquest, war, famine, and pandemic occur — the framework behind the enormously popular *Left Behind* franchise. **Read as symbolic:** Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and mainline Protestant traditions, together with most academic scholarship, take the horsemen as figurative — whether of John's own Roman world (preterist) or of the perennial pattern of catastrophe (idealist). *Survey backdrop:* polling consistently finds that a large share of American Christians — and a clear majority of white evangelicals — expect a literal end-times scenario, and that belief in the Second Coming and a coming tribulation is widespread; the precise figures move with the wording and the year (Pew has tracked this), and there is little story-specific polling on the horsemen themselves. *(Refresh the figures against current Pew/PRRI data at lock.)*

### Older sources & cross-tradition parallels
**Antecedents (within Israel's scriptures).** *(Ancient-World Historian with the Comparative-Religion scholar.)* Unlike a story such as Noah, which reworks a foreign epic, the horsemen are assembled almost entirely out of **earlier biblical material**, and the borrowing is direct.

- **Zechariah's horses.** The prophet **Zechariah** twice sees coloured horses: patrol-horses (red, sorrel, white) ranging "to and fro through the earth" (Zechariah 1:8–11) and four chariots drawn by red, black, white, and dappled horses, identified as "the four winds/spirits of heaven" sent out over the world (Zechariah 6:1–8). John takes the coloured horses and the idea of heaven-dispatched agents straight from here. *Relationship:* **direct reworking** *(well-attested)*.
- **Ezekiel's four judgments.** The fourth horseman's four weapons — sword, famine, pestilence, wild beasts — are, in that order, God's "four deadly judgments" in **Ezekiel 14:21**; the covenant curses of **Leviticus 26** and **Deuteronomy 28** stand further back. *Relationship:* **direct quotation / shared tradition** *(well-attested)*.

**Ancient Near Eastern and Greco-Roman colour.** *(Ancient-World Historian.)* Colour-symbolism for horses and the figure of the mounted archer belong to the wider ancient world, and the white-horse bowman would have evoked **Parthia**, Rome's feared eastern rival, to John's first readers. *Relationship:* **shared cultural furniture and contemporary reference**, not a borrowed text.

**Cousins (other living traditions).** *(Comparative-Religion / Islam Scholar.)* Here this entry differs sharply from a shared story like Cain and Abel: **there is no cross-canon "cousin."** Judaism does not receive Revelation, and the **Qur'an and Hadith**, while rich in eschatology — the signs of the Hour, the false messiah (*al-Dajjāl*), Gog and Magog, the Beast of the Earth — contain **nothing answering to the four horsemen**. *Relationship:* **independent apocalyptic traditions** that share only the broad genre of end-time signs, with no literary link.

### Psychoanalytic reading (the depth-psychology lens)
*One contested lens among many, applied to the images of the vision, not to any person.*

- **Jung: the apocalyptic and the quaternity.** C. G. Jung, who took Revelation seriously as a document of the psyche (*Answer to Job*), was preoccupied with the number **four** as a symbol of wholeness; the four horsemen can be read as a dark **quaternity**, the destructive shadow-side of the self erupting when long repressed — and apocalyptic imagery in general as the collective unconscious breaking through in an age of anxiety. *Grade:* **plausible to imposed.**
- **Apocalypse as projected dread.** More broadly, depth-psychology and psychoanalytic critics read end-times imagery as collective fear given monstrous, externalized form — catastrophe imagined as four riders so that it can be faced as a story. *Grade:* an **applied** reading.

*What to keep in view:* psychoanalysis is itself contested as science, so these are ways of seeing the vision, set beside the historical and theological readings rather than above them.

### Schisms, sects & ecclesial crises (what this reading split off)
**Largely n/a** *(Schism & Sectarian-Formation Historian).* No church division turned on the four horsemen specifically. ### Cultural Afterlife (impact on the world)

- **Art:** **Albrecht Dürer's** woodcut *The Four Horsemen* (from the *Apocalypse* series, 1498) is the defining image and among the most reproduced prints in Western art; the riders also fill medieval apocalypse cycles (the Bamberg Apocalypse, the Angers tapestry) and later works by Benjamin West and Viktor Vasnetsov (1887).
- **Literature:** Vicente Blasco Ibáñez's best-selling novel *The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse* (1916) carried the image into the modern popular imagination; D. H. Lawrence's *Apocalypse* (1931) is a sustained meditation on the book.
- **Music:** Metallica's "The Four Horsemen," the Greek progressive-rock album *666* by Aphrodite's Child, and Johnny Cash's "The Man Comes Around," which opens by quoting Revelation 6.
- **Film, TV & stage:** the 1921 film of Ibáñez's novel that made Rudolph Valentino a star (remade 1962); *Pale Rider* (1985); the villain Apocalypse and his Four Horsemen in the *X-Men* franchise; personified horsemen in *Good Omens* and *Supernatural*.
- **Language & idiom:** **"the four horsemen of the apocalypse"** for any quartet of looming threats, and the productive **"four horsemen of X"** — the **New Atheists** (Dawkins, Dennett, Harris, Hitchens, who adopted the label) and psychologist **John Gottman's** "four horsemen" of relationship breakdown (criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling).
- **Politics, law & social movements:** "war, famine, pestilence, and death" survives as a rhetorical quartet for any compounding crisis; the "pale horse" is invoked in writing about pandemics and mass death.
- **Sports:** **Grantland Rice's** 1924 description of Notre Dame's backfield — "the Four Horsemen rode again" — is among the most famous leads in sportswriting and fixed the phrase in American culture.
- **Harmful afterlife** *(Reception-History Harm Historian).* The harm here is real but diffuse, and lighter than a text directly weaponized against a people. The vision's own controls matter first: the riders are **let out by the Lamb**, their reach is **bounded** ("a quarter of the earth"), and nothing in the text **welcomes** the catastrophe or assigns it to any group.

  **Apocalyptic fatalism and the welcoming of catastrophe.** *Text.* Rev 6:1–8 — symbolic, bounded judgment under God's authority. *Reception.* Read as a literal, inevitable end-times script, the horsemen have fed a fatalism in which war, ecological collapse, or pandemic are treated as **foreordained and not to be resisted** — even, at the fringe, as developments to be hastened. *Consequence.* Diffuse rather than institutional: end-times panic, date-setting and its disappointments, and a quietism about real-world suffering. *(documented; limited scope)* *Counter-tradition.* The mainstream churches' symbolic and "already/not-yet" readings explicitly reject treating the vision as a timetable or an excuse for passivity. *Status.* **Framed as a misreading.**

  **"Pestilence" rhetoric in epidemics.** *Text.* The pale rider's plague is one bounded judgment among four. *Reception.* In times of plague the "pale horse" and "pestilence" have been pulled into **apocalyptic scapegoating** of minorities and outsiders. *Consequence.* Real but episodic, riding on older prejudices rather than originating them. *Status.* **No basis in the text; framed as misuse.**
