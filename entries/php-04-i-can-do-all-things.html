THE READING

1 · The text

A faithful translation made directly from the Greek, kept close to the wording so the famous last line stays in view of the four verses that set it up. The most quoted sentence in the New Testament's motivational afterlife — "I can do all things" — is verse 13, and almost no one who quotes it has read verses 11 and 12.

10 I rejoiced greatly in the Lord that now, at last, you have made your care for me bloom again — you were caring all along, but you had no chance to show it. 11 I do not say this because I am in need, for I have learned, whatever my circumstances, to be content. 12 I know what it is to be brought low, and I know what it is to have plenty; in any and every situation I have learned the secret — being well fed and going hungry, having more than enough and going without. 13 I have strength for all of it through the one who gives me strength.

2 · How the translations differ — and what's at stake

These four verses look simple, but verse 13 hides two quiet forks, and the English you happen to have read has already chosen for you at both.

  • "Through Christ," or just "through him"? This is a real difference in the ancient manuscripts. The oldest and best copies of the letter read only "through him who strengthens me" — they do not name Christ; the name is understood from everything around it. Later copyists, wanting to be helpful, wrote the name in, and that fuller text is what the King James Version (1611) translated: "I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me." The ASV (1901), following the older manuscripts, prints "I can do all things in him that strengtheneth me" — no "Christ." The modern World English Bible keeps the name: "I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me." On the standard text-critical reading the meaning is unchanged — the "him" was always understood as Christ — but it is a clean, visible example of how a famous wording grew: the most-quoted half of the verse contains a word the apostle very likely did not write.
  • "Do all things," or "have strength for all this"? This is the fork that matters. The Greek verb behind "I can do" (ischyō) does not mean accomplish or achieve; it means to be strong, to have the strength for. And "all things" (panta) is not open-ended — it is governed by the sentence just before it, which is entirely about being full and hungry, having plenty and having nothing. So the contextual sense is closer to "I have the strength to face all of it" — every circumstance, the lows as much as the highs — than to "I can do anything I set my mind to." The King James "I can do all things" is not wrong, but four centuries of readers have heard it as a promise of limitless capability, lifted clean out of a paragraph about learning to be content with little. The translation above leans into the context; most printed bibles keep the open-sounding "all things," which is exactly why the verse travels so well on a wristband. (The scholarly point is the framing, not a claim that "all things" is grammatically impossible — it is that the surrounding verses tell you which sense Paul meant.)
  • A word with a philosopher's pedigree (v. 11). "Content" translates autarkēs, which in Paul's world was a technical term of the Stoic and Cynic philosophers — the self-sufficiency of the wise person who needs nothing outside themselves to be at peace (see Older sources). Paul reaches for the famous word and then, in verse 13, turns it inside out.
  • "I have learned the secret" (v. 12). The verb here (memyēmai) literally means "I have been initiated" — the language of the mystery religions, where initiates were let in on a hidden rite. The King James renders it "I am instructed"; the flavor in the Greek is of someone admitted to a secret, which is why "I have learned the secret of…" has become the common modern rendering.

3 · Plain-English retelling

(This is a letter, not a story, so the "retelling" is a plain paraphrase of what Paul is actually saying — the way he might put it to a friend.)

Paul is writing from prison. (He is in chains somewhere in the Roman world — the letter says so plainly — and writing to a small church he had founded years earlier in Philippi, a Roman town in northern Greece. They had just sent him money and supplies to help him survive his imprisonment, carried by one of their own, a man named Epaphroditus who nearly died on the trip.) Near the end of the letter, he turns to the awkward business of saying thank you — awkward because in the ancient world a gift created a debt, and Paul does not want to sound like a man fishing for more.

So he thanks them, and then immediately makes something clear. I was overjoyed, he says, that your care for me has flowered again — I know you were thinking of me all along; you just had no way to act on it. And then the careful part: Don't think I'm telling you this because I was desperate.

Because, he says, he has learned something. Whatever state he is in, he has learned to be content. He knows what it is to have nothing, and he knows what it is to have plenty; he has been hungry and he has been full; he has done without and he has had more than enough. He has been let in, as he puts it, on a secret — and the secret is that none of those conditions, high or low, has the power to undo him.

And then the line everyone knows: I have the strength for all of it — for the abundance and the emptiness alike — through the one who gives me strength.

He is not saying he can do anything he wants. He is sitting in a cell, thanking people for a care package, and telling them he would have been all right either way — that the source of his steadiness is not his circumstances, and not even his own famous toughness, but something he locates outside himself.

4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)

The passage is about contentment — specifically, contentment that does not depend on how things are going. Paul sets up a perfect balance: he can handle being full, and he can handle being empty; the danger in each is different (need can crush you; plenty can own you), and he claims to have learned his way through both. That is the "secret" he says he was initiated into.

What makes verse 13 more than a self-help slogan is where Paul says the strength comes from. The word he uses for contentment in verse 11, autarkēs, was the Stoic ideal of self-sufficiency — the wise person so inwardly complete they need nothing and no one. Paul borrows the prestige word and then, on the scholarly reading, quietly detonates it: his sufficiency is not self-sufficiency. The strength to be content "in any and every situation" comes "through the one who gives me strength." He keeps the Stoic poise and relocates its source from inside the self to outside it. That is the whole move of the passage, and it is the opposite of the modern reading, which puts the verse on a poster to mean you are limitless.

So the popular use gets it almost exactly backwards. "I can do all things" is quoted to promise achievement, victory, success — but Paul writes it from a prison cell, about having learned to need less, in a sentence whose other half is "and I know how to go hungry." The verse is not a key that unlocks anything you want. It is a claim that there is a kind of steadiness available that the loss of everything cannot reach.

5 · Why this matters

This is one of the most quoted, printed, worn, and tattooed verses in the modern English-speaking world — and one of the most reliably misquoted, which is exactly why it is worth knowing in full. If you have seen "I can do all things through Christ" on a graduation card, a gym wall, a coffee mug, an athlete's eye-black, or the side of a sneaker, you have met Philippians 4:13. It was named YouVersion's Verse of the Year for 2013. The college quarterback Tim Tebow wore "PHIL 4:13" in his eye-black through his 2008 season, and wore "JOHN 3:16" in the January 2009 national-championship game; the basketball star Stephen Curry has carried "I can do all things…" on his shoes. In almost all of these settings the verse means I can achieve anything — push through, win, overcome — though some of that everyday use (strength to endure a hard season, to keep going) lands closer to Paul's own sense than the achievement reading does; the sharp misuse is the prosperity claim that faith guarantees the win, treated below.

Knowing the passage lets you see the gap between that use and the source. The line is not about achievement; it is the climax of a paragraph about learning to be content whether you have plenty or nothing, written by a man in prison thanking friends for a gift he insists he didn't strictly need. That gap is not a trivial "gotcha." It is the difference between a verse that promises you'll win and a verse that promises you'll be all right even if you lose — a much older and stranger idea, and the one Paul actually wrote. It also gives you a foothold on a whole modern argument: the same verse sits at the center of the "prosperity gospel," the movement that reads scripture as a guarantee of health and wealth, and watching how 4:13 gets used is a quick lesson in how a text can be turned to mean the reverse of its plain setting. Finally, it is a doorway to a real historical fact most people never notice — that the New Testament was written inside the Greek philosophical world, close enough to Stoicism that Paul could pick up the Stoics' favorite word for self-sufficiency and argue with it.

BEHIND THE TEXT

Setting & Context

When (in the story): late in Paul's career, during an imprisonment — the letter repeatedly refers to his "chains" (1:13) and reckons with the possibility of execution (1:20–23). When (scholarly): Philippians is one of the seven undisputed letters of Paul, so its authenticity is not in question; the debate is over which imprisonment. The traditional setting is Rome, c. 60–62 CE (supported by the letter's references to the praetorian guard and "Caesar's household," 1:13; 4:22 — though both terms also existed in provincial capitals); many scholars argue instead for an earlier Ephesian imprisonment (c. 54–55 CE), which fits the easy back-and-forth travel the letter assumes, and a minority for Caesarea. Where: Philippi, a Roman colonia in Macedonia (northern Greece) on the Via Egnatia, the great east–west highway. It was the site of the 42 BCE Battle of Philippi, where Antony and Octavian defeated Caesar's assassins Brutus and Cassius; afterward it was settled with Roman veterans, which is why its church thinks of itself in Roman terms. Paul founded the congregation on his first push into Europe (Acts 16): his first convert there was Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth, and the visit included Paul being beaten and jailed — and, when the magistrates learned they had flogged a Roman citizen uncondemned, publicly apologizing (Acts 16:37–39) — so a letter about contentment in prison, to people who first knew him in their town's prison, has a private resonance. Background a modern reader needs: in the ancient Mediterranean a gift created a social debt and a relationship of obligation; accepting money could compromise a teacher's independence, and wandering philosophers were often suspected of being in it for the cash. Paul is walking that tightrope here — accepting the Philippians' support warmly while insisting he is not dependent on it. The church had sent help before (4:15–16) and now again, via Epaphroditus, who carried their gift to Paul and fell gravely ill in the process (2:25–30). Cast: Paul — the imprisoned apostle, writing the letter. The Philippian church — his beloved, financially loyal congregation, the recipients. Epaphroditus — their messenger, who brought the gift and nearly died. Timothy — Paul's co-sender and protégé (1:1).

Text & Composition

Key original-language terms. Autarkēs (4:11), "content" — the Stoic/Cynic technical term for self-sufficiency (autarkeia), the inner completeness of the sage; Paul adopts the word and then re-roots its source outside the self (see The Reading §2 and Older sources). Memyēmai (4:12), "I have learned the secret" — literally "I have been initiated," a verb from the mystery cults. Panta ischyō (4:13), "I can do all things" — ischyō is "to have strength for," not "to accomplish," and panta ("all things") is governed by the abundance-and-want context of v. 12. En tō endynamounti me, "through the one who strengthens me" — the participle is from dynamis, "power"; the later manuscript tradition specifies the unnamed strengthener as "Christ." Textual notes & variants. The one substantive variant in the unit is the added Χριστῷ ("Christ") at 4:13: absent from the earliest and best witnesses (P46, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus), present in the later Byzantine majority and so in the Textus Receptus behind the KJV. It is a clarifying scribal addition, not a change of sense (the antecedent was always Christ). The reader-facing version of this is walked through in The Reading §2 and not repeated here. Authorship & date (traditional): Paul, writing from Rome near the end of his life (c. 60–62 CE). Authorship & date (scholarly): genuinely Pauline and undisputed; dated by which imprisonment one favors (Rome c. 60–62, or Ephesus c. 54–55). A long-running partition debate asks whether canonical Philippians is a single letter or an editor's stitching of two or three Pauline notes — with 4:10–20, the thank-you, sometimes proposed as a separate, earlier "receipt" later appended. The theory is real but contested; partition has prominent defenders (e.g., Walter Schmithals, and earlier source-critics), while many scholars (e.g., Gordon Fee) defend the letter's unity. Either way the words are Paul's.

Is it historical?

Unlike the primeval stories of Genesis, this is not a question of whether a legendary event "really happened" — it is a document, and a securely historical one. What the text claims: that the apostle Paul, imprisoned, wrote to thank a church in Philippi and described his own learned contentment. What the evidence shows: Paul is among the best-attested individuals of the first century, known from his own undisputed letters (primary sources) and from Acts; Philippi is an extensively excavated Roman colony whose forum, theater, and later churches are visible today; the letter itself is the evidence for the rest. What scholars conclude: the letter is authentically Paul's and authentically first-century; the contents are a real person's real correspondence, not a later legend. The only genuinely open historical questions are secondary — the place and date of the imprisonment behind it (Rome vs. Ephesus vs. Caesarea), and whether the canonical letter was edited together from more than one note. Grade — as a genuine first-century letter of Paul to a real church: well-attested (A4 — the author is historically secure and the place is excavated; only the imprisonment's provenance and date are debated, which is why it is not a flat A5). The interior claim — that Paul personally felt content in want and plenty — is not the sort of thing evidence can test, but the frame around it is solid history.

Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted

The historic Christian reading — across Greek, Latin, and Reformation interpreters — took the verse as being about endurance and contentment, the strength to bear any condition. John Chrysostom (4th c.) read it as Paul's freedom from the tyranny of circumstances; Augustine made the verse a cornerstone of his case against Pelagius — that the strength to do good is itself God's gift, not the soul's own power (a reading that turns 4:13 directly against self-sufficiency); in the Latin tradition Thomas Aquinas, in his lectures on Philippians, took the strength as Christ's grace enabling Paul to bear both abasement and abundance; John Calvin glossed it as the power to be "abased" and to "abound" without being mastered by either. On this, Catholic, Orthodox, and the mainline Protestant traditions broadly agree: the verse is about sufficiency-in-Christ under pressure, not a promise of success. Academic / exegetical reading: modern commentators (Gordon Fee, Markus Bockmuehl, and others) stress the grammar and the context — ischyō as "have strength for," panta as bounded by vv. 11–12 — and read the unit as Paul's deliberate engagement with the Greco-Roman ideal of autarkeia (see Older sources). This is the contextual reading set against the popular one. The modern popular reading: in the 20th and 21st centuries the verse detached from its paragraph and became a standalone motto of empowerment and achievement — the form in which most people now meet it. This is the sharpest "shift" in the unit's history: not a quarrel between churches, but a drift between the scholarly/contextual sense and the devotional/motivational one, accelerated by sports culture, greeting cards, and the prosperity movement (below).

Allegorical reading (figural & typological senses)

n/a — a first-century thank-you note has no significant allegorical or typological tradition; it is read for its plain paraenetic (exhortational) sense. (The field does real work on narrative and visionary texts — the Red Sea crossing, the Song of Songs, Revelation — not on Paul's occasional ethics.)

Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)

This axis barely applies in its usual form: no one disputes the historicity of Paul writing the line, so there is no literal-vs-symbolic fight of the kind that surrounds the miracles or the primeval history. The live divide is instead one of scope — how far the "all things" reaches: - The bounded / contextual reading (contentment in any circumstance) is held by biblical scholarship across the board and by the historic interpretive tradition, and is taught in most mainline and confessional churches. - The maximal / empowerment reading (strength to achieve anything) dominates popular devotional culture and is pressed hardest by the Word-of-Faith and prosperity-gospel movements, where it functions as a guarantee of success. This is less a denominational doctrine than a populist usage that cuts across groups, strongest in American evangelical and Pentecostal-adjacent settings. (This field is owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona; there is essentially no verse-specific survey data on how readers take Philippians 4:13, so the split is described qualitatively rather than with figures.)

Parallel Accounts, Comparison & Harmonization

Older sources & cross-tradition parallels

Antecedents (the Greco-Roman world). (Ancient-World Historian.) The decisive background here is not the ancient Near East but Greek moral philosophy. Autarkeia — "self-sufficiency" — was a central ideal of the Stoics and Cynics: the sage (and Diogenes the Cynic as its proverbial emblem) needs nothing external to be happy, and is therefore untouchable by fortune. Seneca and Epictetus, near-contemporaries of Paul, work the same ground — the free person is the one no circumstance can disturb. Paul plainly borrows the vocabulary (autarkēs) and the posture (equally at home with plenty and with want). Relationship: shared philosophical vocabulary, deliberately appropriated and transformed (the lexical borrowing is well-attested; whether Paul knew Stoic teaching directly or absorbed it from the common culture is debated) — and the transformation is the point: Paul keeps Stoic imperturbability but relocates its source from the self to Christ, so his is a contentment that is received rather than self-generated. He is not adopting Stoicism; he is arguing with it in its own words. (Troels Engberg-Pedersen, Paul and the Stoics.) Cousins (other living traditions). (Comparative-Religion Scholar.) There is no shared-scripture parallel of the Qur'anic kind here — this is a personal letter, not a story Islam retells. But the virtue the passage commends has close conceptual cousins in Islamic ethics: qanāʿa (contentment with what God provides), riḍā (glad acceptance of God's decree), and tawakkul (reliant trust in God) describe the same inner steadiness rooted in God rather than circumstances. A widely cited saying (Bukhārī, Muslim) puts the same point directly — true richness is not abundance of possessions but contentment of the soul (al-ghinā ghinā al-nafs) — a convergent ideal, not a borrowed text. Relationship: independent parallel (no textual dependence; a convergent religious ideal of God-grounded contentment that several traditions reach on their own — well-attested as a parallel of virtue, not of text).

Schisms, sects & ecclesial crises (what this reading split off)

n/a (Schism & Sectarian-Formation Historian) — no church division or sect turned on the reading of Philippians 4:13. The prosperity-gospel usage is a reception phenomenon and a documented misuse (see the Harmful afterlife block), not an ecclesial schism in the technical sense.

Connections

Echoed / related elsewhere: within the same letter, Philippians 4:6–7 ("do not be anxious… the peace of God") and the great Christ-hymn of 2:5–11 ("he emptied himself"), whose self-emptying Christ is the model for the contentment here; 4:19 ("my God will supply all your needs"), the next beat of the same thank-you. Beyond it, 2 Corinthians 12:9–10 ("my power is made perfect in weakness… when I am weak, then I am strong") is the closest Pauline twin; 1 Timothy 6:6–8 ("godliness with contentment is great gain… having food and clothing, we will be content") states the same ethic; the strength-in-weakness theme runs back to the lament Psalms. Themes & motifs: contentment; sufficiency; strength-in-weakness; joy under suffering; dependence on God rather than circumstances; the right use of plenty and of need. Related entries in this project: the Christ-hymn (Philippians 2:5–11, the same letter's theological center); Paul's missionary journeys (Acts 16, the founding of the Philippian church); the hymn to love (1 Corinthians 13) and strength in weakness (2 Corinthians 12) as neighboring Pauline set-pieces.

Cultural Afterlife (impact on the world)

  • Language & idiom: "I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me" is a free-standing phrase of encouragement in English, detached from the letter — said and stitched and printed as a charm against difficulty. It is among the handful of Bible verses most people can complete from memory.
  • Sports: the verse is a fixture of American athletic devotion. Tim Tebow wore "PHIL 4:13" in his eye-black during the 2008 college-football season; Stephen Curry has carried "I can do all things…" on his signature sneakers. It recurs on wristbands, cleats, and locker-room walls across football and basketball.
  • Music: the reference recurs in gospel and Christian-rap song titles and lyrics as shorthand for resilience; it is a common track and album title rather than the subject of one landmark setting.
  • Popular & material culture: one of the most common scripture tattoos, and a perennial on graduation cards, mugs, posters, phone cases, and gym merchandise; it was named YouVersion's Verse of the Year for 2013 (its top verse by combined bookmarks, highlights, and shares; the single most-shared verse that year was Psalm 118:24). (In several recent years the top spot has gone to the nearby Philippians 4:6, "do not be anxious" — a useful reminder that 4:13's dominance is real but not permanent.)
  • Harmful afterlife (Reception-History Harm Historian). Philippians 4:13 carries a bounded, well-documented misuse, recorded here only as misuse, framed and rejected. The text's own control comes first: Paul writes the line from prison, about having learned to go hungry, and its plain subject is contentment in want, not success. The verse as a guarantee of achievement and prosperity. Text. Phil 4:10–13 — strength to be content in plenty and in want; the "all things" is bounded by hunger and abundance, and the strength is received "through the one who strengthens me." Reception. In modern motivational and especially Word-of-Faith / prosperity-gospel settings the verse is lifted from its paragraph and read as a blanket promise that the believer can accomplish, win, or obtain anything with enough faith (Kate Bowler, Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel, traces the broader movement this belongs to). Consequence. The reading amplified an existing prosperity theology and produced real pastoral harm: where success is promised, failure, illness, poverty, or defeat get read back as a deficiency of faith, heaping shame on people already suffering — a move many readers note runs directly against the book of Job, which confronts exactly the claim that suffering proves a deficiency of faith. The scope is personal and devotional, not tied to law or mass violence. (well-attested as a pattern; the harm is real but bounded.) Counter-tradition. The verse's own context is the rebuttal — it is a statement about needing less, written by a hungry prisoner — and mainstream scholarship and most churches read it that way. Status. A misreading against the passage's plain setting; framed and rejected.
