## THE READING

### 1 · The text

*The four Gospels are harmonized here into one narrative, with the major divergences flagged as they arise — because the differences between the accounts are, for this passage above all, part of the point. All quotations are from public-domain translations (KJV, ASV, WEB).*

**The road to Golgotha.** Jesus is led out to "the place of a skull" — **Golgotha** (Aramaic; the Latin *calvaria* gives "Calvary"). On the way, the soldiers compel **Simon of Cyrene** to carry the cross (Mark 15:21; Matt 27:32; Luke 23:26); Mark alone names Simon's sons, Alexander and Rufus, as if they were known to his readers. *Divergence:* **John (19:17) says Jesus carried the cross "by himself"** — no Simon — fitting John's portrait of a Jesus sovereignly in control. Luke alone has Jesus turn to the weeping "daughters of Jerusalem."

**The crucifixion.** They offer him wine drugged with **myrrh** (Mark 15:23) — Matthew has **gall** (27:34, echoing Psalm 69:21) — which he does not take. He is crucified between two others, "robbers" or "criminals," and the soldiers divide his garments, **casting lots** for them — the narrator pointing to Psalm 22:18 ("they part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture"). Over his head hangs the *titulus*, the placard of his crime.

**The inscription (INRI).** All four record it; the wording differs. Mark: "THE KING OF THE JEWS." Luke: "THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS." Matthew: "THIS IS JESUS THE KING OF THE JEWS." John: "JESUS OF NAZARETH THE KING OF THE JEWS" — whose Latin, *Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum*, gives the acronym **INRI**. *John alone* adds that it was written in three languages (Hebrew/Aramaic, Latin, Greek) and that when the priests objected, Pilate answered, "What I have written, I have written."

**The mocking and the darkness.** Passers-by, the rulers, and the soldiers jeer — "He saved others; himself he cannot save." In Mark and Matthew **both** crucified men revile him; *Luke alone* turns one into the **penitent thief**, to whom Jesus promises paradise. From the sixth to the ninth hour (noon to about three) **darkness** covers the land (Mark, Matthew, Luke; John omits it).

**The seven last words** — no single Gospel has all of them; this is the great harmonization:
1. *"Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do"* (Luke 23:34 — **text-critically uncertain**; absent from several early manuscripts).
2. *"Today you will be with me in paradise"* (Luke 23:43, to the penitent thief).
3. *"Woman, behold your son … behold your mother"* (John 19:26–27, to Mary and the beloved disciple).
4. *"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"* — the **cry of dereliction**, given in Aramaic/Hebrew (*Eloi/Eli … sabachthani*), quoting Psalm 22:1 (Mark 15:34; Matt 27:46).
5. *"I thirst"* (John 19:28).
6. *"It is finished"* — *tetelestai* (John 19:30).
7. *"Father, into your hands I commit my spirit"* (Luke 23:46, quoting Psalm 31:5).
   **The death — three different keys.** The accounts do not merely differ in detail; they portray strikingly different deaths. **Mark** is starkest: Jesus' only word from the cross is the cry of abandonment, then a wordless loud cry, and he dies. **Luke** is serene: he forgives, reassures the thief, and dies trusting — "into your hands." **John** is sovereign: he settles his mother's care, declares "It is finished," and *gives up* his spirit, in command to the end. He dies; the **veil of the Temple** is torn from top to bottom (Mark, Matthew, Luke). *Matthew alone* (27:51–53) adds an **earthquake**, splitting rocks, opening tombs, and risen saints. The **centurion** confesses — "Truly this man was the Son of God" (Mark, Matthew) or "Surely this was a righteous man" (Luke). Women watch from a distance.

**John's spear.** *John alone* (19:31–37) reports that the soldiers broke the others' legs to hasten death but, finding Jesus already dead, did not break his — "that the scripture should be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken" (Exodus 12; the Passover lamb) — and one pierced his side with a spear, "and forthwith came there out blood and water" (fulfilling Zechariah 12:10, "they shall look on him whom they pierced").

**The burial.** **Joseph of Arimathea** asks Pilate for the body and lays it in a new, unused tomb. *John adds* **Nicodemus**, with a large weight of myrrh and aloes. *Matthew alone* adds that the authorities set a **guard** at the tomb.

### 2 · How the translations differ — and what's at stake

The crucifixion is told in plain narrative, but several words carry the weight of centuries:

- **Golgotha / Calvary.** All four call the site "the place of a skull." "Golgotha" transliterates the Aramaic; "**Calvary**" comes only from the Latin Vulgate's *calvaria*, "skull" — so the two famous names are the same word in two languages, not two places.
- **The inscription — INRI.** The wording genuinely differs across the four (above). The Latin of John's fullest form yields **INRI**; Eastern Orthodox icons instead use the Greek **INBI**, or replace it with "King of Glory." Apologists propose each evangelist quotes a different one of the three languages; critical scholars simply note the tradition never fixed the exact words. That a *titulus* named the crime is itself historically plausible (it was standard Roman practice), whatever the precise text.
- **The cry of dereliction — and why they thought he called Elijah.** Matthew transliterates *Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani*; Mark has *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani*. The word for "forsake" (*sabachthani*) is **Aramaic**; the bystanders' confusion — "he calleth for **Elias** [Elijah]" — works because "Eli/Eloi" sounds like the prophet's Hebrew name. The line quotes the opening of **Psalm 22**, a lament that *ends* in vindication and praise — which is why readers split over whether the cry is pure despair or a pointer to the whole psalm.
- **"It is finished" — *tetelestai*.** The Greek (John 19:30) is the perfect of *teleō*, "to complete, accomplish": his work and the scriptures are *brought to their end*. A popular claim that it was a commercial term meaning "paid in full" rests on a confusion with a different word on tax receipts; the mainstream reading, held since the Church Fathers, is *completion*, not a bookkeeping metaphor.
- **"The Jews" (especially in John).** John repeatedly attributes the pressure for Jesus' death to *hoi Ioudaioi*, "the Jews." Whether to render this "the Jews," "the Judeans," or "the Jewish authorities" is now a live and consequential translation question, because the broad rendering fed the deicide charge (see *Harmful afterlife*). Most scholars stress that John's own community was Jewish and that the term marks a specific intra-Jewish conflict, not a whole people for all time.
### 3 · Plain-English retelling

They take Jesus out of the city to a place called Golgotha — "the skull." He has been flogged and is failing, so the soldiers grab a man out of the crowd, Simon from Cyrene, and load the crossbeam onto him to carry the rest of the way. (John's account skips this and has Jesus carry it himself — one of many small differences between the four tellings.)

At the site they offer him drugged wine, which he refuses, and nail him to the cross between two condemned criminals. The soldiers, as executioners did, take his clothes and gamble for them. Above his head they fix the charge that has killed him: **King of the Jews** — the title that turned a Galilean preacher into a Roman political threat. People file past and mock him: *he saved others, let him save himself.* In Mark's and Matthew's versions even the two criminals join in; Luke tells it differently — one of them defends Jesus and is promised a place in paradise that very day.

Around noon the sky goes dark for three hours. And here the four accounts give us four different deaths. In Mark — the oldest and barest — Jesus cries out the terrible words of Psalm 22, *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?*, gives a wordless shout, and dies, utterly alone. In Luke he is calm to the end: he forgives his executioners, comforts the dying thief, and hands himself over — *Father, into your hands I commit my spirit.* In John he is never out of control: he arranges for his mother's care, says simply *It is finished*, and releases his own life. The same death, remembered three ways.

At the moment he dies, the great curtain in the Temple rips from top to bottom. Matthew alone piles on the cosmic signs — an earthquake, tombs breaking open. The Roman officer in charge, watching it all, says the line the Gospel has been building toward: *truly this was the Son of God* (or, in Luke, *surely this man was innocent*). The women who had followed him watch from a distance. In John, to make sure he is dead, a soldier drives a spear into his side, and blood and water flow out. Then a sympathizer with standing, Joseph of Arimathea, gets permission to take the body down and lays it in a fresh tomb cut into rock — and the longest day in the Christian calendar is over.

### 4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)

On the surface this is an execution — a specific, brutal Roman one. To grasp the story you have to grasp what **crucifixion was**: not a generic death penalty but the most degrading one Rome had, the "slave's punishment," staged in public to humiliate and to terrorize, reserved for rebels, slaves, and the conquered. For a movement to claim that its founder *and Lord* died this way was, in its own world, an absurdity and a scandal — which is part of why historians are so confident it happened (see *Is it historical?*).

For Christianity, though, the cross is not a defeat but the hinge of everything. The claim — worked out in a dozen ways the traditions still argue over (see *Interpretation*) — is that this death *did something*: that in it God dealt with human sin and death from the inside. Hence the otherwise strange fact that Christians took an instrument of torture and made it their central symbol, the way you might wear a tiny electric chair around your neck — except transfigured, because the story does not end at the grave.

And the four-fold telling is itself meaningful. The Gospels were not embarrassed to remember the death four different ways — abandoned (Mark), forgiving (Luke), triumphant (John) — because each is saying something about who they believed was dying and why. The differences are not a bug to be harmonized away; they are four theologians at work.

### 5 · Why this matters

This is the source of the most recognizable symbol on earth. The **cross** — and its filled-in form the **crucifix** — marks churches, graves, flags, and bodies across the world. (A quiet denominational tell: most **Protestants** use an *empty* cross, stressing the resurrection; **Catholics and Orthodox** use a **crucifix** bearing the body, stressing the sacrifice.) The vocabulary is everywhere: "**a cross to bear**," "**crucify him**," "**Calvary**," "**Golgotha**," "the **stations of the cross**," and the four letters **INRI** on a thousand paintings.

It is the engine of Western sacred art and music. The crucifixion is, with the Nativity, the most-depicted subject in the history of Western art — Grünewald's harrowing **Isenheim Altarpiece**, Velázquez, Rubens, Dalí's vertiginous **Christ of Saint John of the Cross**, and Chagall's **White Crucifixion**, which paints Jesus as a murdered Jew amid the pogroms. It gave the world Bach's **St Matthew** and **St John Passions**, Handel's **Messiah**, and the spiritual **"Were You There When They Crucified My Lord."** And beyond religion, "the cross" became the culture's shorthand for **redemptive or innocent suffering** — the figure of the one who suffers unjustly and somehow makes meaning of it.

Knowing this story lets you read a Renaissance altarpiece, understand what a Christian means by "the cross" or "atonement," follow an argument about a Passion play or a Mel Gibson film, and recognize why a Roman torture device became, against every expectation, a sign of hope. It also — and this matters — lets you see how the same story was turned into a weapon against the people Jesus belonged to (see *Harmful afterlife*).

## BEHIND THE TEXT

### Setting & Context

**When (in the story):** a Friday at Passover, in the prefecture of Pontius Pilate — the climax of the final week.
**When (scholarly):** the *event* is dated with confidence to Pilate's tenure (26–36 CE); the *year* is debated, with **c. 30 CE** (most scholars; Friday 7 April 30) the leading view and **33 CE** (Friday 3 April 33) the main alternative.
**Where:** **Golgotha**, "the place of a skull," just outside the walls of Jerusalem; the traditional site is enshrined in the **Church of the Holy Sepulchre**.
**Background a modern reader needs:** **crucifixion** was a Roman method of execution by prolonged public torture, the *summum supplicium* — the supreme penalty — used for slaves, pirates, rebels, and non-citizens, and avoided even in polite speech by Roman writers (Martin Hengel's classic study documents its sheer shamefulness). A *titulus* naming the condemned man's crime was standard. The charge against Jesus — "King of the Jews" — is a **political** one: a claimant to kingship was a threat to Rome, which is why the penalty was Rome's, not the Temple's.
**Cast:** **Jesus**; **Pontius Pilate**, the Roman prefect who orders the execution; **Simon of Cyrene**, pressed to carry the cross; the **two criminals** crucified alongside (one penitent in Luke); the **centurion** who confesses; **the women** — Mary his mother, Mary Magdalene, and others — watching; **Joseph of Arimathea** and (in John) **Nicodemus**, who bury him.

### Text & Composition

**The words are doing precise work.** *Golgotha* / *calvaria* (skull); the *titulus* (INRI); the Aramaic *sabachthani* of the cry of dereliction; *tetelestai* ("it is finished," John 19:30); John's *hoi Ioudaioi* ("the Jews / Judeans"). The reader-facing cruxes are walked through in §2.

**The earliest connected story.** Scholars widely hold that the **passion narrative was the first extended, sequential narrative** the early Church told — a connected account from the arrest to the grave that circulated before the Gospels were written. On the consensus of **Markan priority**, **Mark** (c. 70 CE) is the earliest; **Matthew** and **Luke** use Mark; **John** preserves a **substantially independent** passion tradition (only loose overlap with the Synoptics).

**Four theologies of one death.** Redaction criticism reads each evangelist's shaping:
- **Mark** — abandonment and the messianic secret; the cry of dereliction is the only word from the cross.
- **Matthew** — apocalyptic portents (earthquake, risen saints) and fulfilment-of-scripture citations.
- **Luke** — a martyr's serenity: forgiveness, the penitent thief, trust.
- **John** — exaltation: the cross as Jesus being "lifted up" and glorified, sovereign throughout.
  **Scripture as the narrative's grammar.** The accounts are saturated with **Psalm 22** (forsakenness, divided garments, mockery), **Psalm 31** (commending the spirit), **Psalm 69** (the gall), and **Isaiah 53** (the Suffering Servant), plus Zechariah 12:10 and the Maccabean martyr traditions. This generates the central composition debate: **"prophecy historicized" (John Dominic Crossan)** — the details spun out of scripture rather than remembered — versus **"history remembered" (Raymond Brown, N. T. Wright)** — scripture used to interpret events that happened. Mark Goodacre's middle term is "scripturalization." Brown's two-volume *The Death of the Messiah* (1994) is the major reference; even Brown judged Matthew's "blood" verse non-historical.

**Authorship & date:** anonymous Gospels conventionally dated c. 70 (Mark), 80–90 (Matthew, Luke), 90–100 (John).

### Is it historical?

**The honest answer — and it is unusual for this corpus: yes. The crucifixion of Jesus under Pilate is among the best-attested facts of ancient history. This is the basis of the entry's `A4`.** (What is *not* a matter of historical demonstration is the theological meaning, the supernatural signs — darkness, earthquake, risen saints — and the verbatim accuracy of the sayings; and the Resurrection is a separate question of faith, treated in its own entry.)

**Why historians are confident:**
- **The criterion of embarrassment.** A crucified messiah was a scandal no one would invent. Crucifixion was Rome's most shameful punishment; claiming it for one's founder cuts against every interest of the early movement — so it is very unlikely to be fiction.
- **Non-Christian attestation.** **Tacitus** (*Annals* 15.44, c. 116 CE) reports that "Christus" "suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of … Pontius Pilatus" — independent and widely accepted as authentic (though Tacitus loosely calls Pilate "procurator"; he was prefect). **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 18.63–64, the *Testimonium Flavianum*) reports the condemnation under Pilate; the majority view is **partial authenticity** — an authentic core with later Christian insertions (e.g. "He was the Christ") — while a minority argues for wholesale interpolation; Josephus's separate reference to "James, the brother of Jesus called Christ" (*Ant.* 20.9.1) is more widely accepted. **Lucian** (satirically, c. 165 CE) and the Syriac letter of **Mara bar Serapion** add further outside notice; the **Talmud** (Sanhedrin 43a) records an execution "on the eve of Passover," though it is late and of limited independent value.
- **Archaeology.** The single best physical evidence is the heel bone of **Yehohanan**, found in 1968 at **Giv'at ha-Mivtar** near Jerusalem, with an iron nail ~11.5 cm still lodged in it (bent on a knot in the wood, so it could not be withdrawn) — published by Vassilios Tzaferis (*Israel Exploration Journal*, 1970) and re-assessed by Zias and Sekeles (1985). It confirms that first-century crucifixion victims could be nailed *and* given burial.
  **Open questions, named:**
- **The medical cause of death** (asphyxiation, hypovolemic shock, cardiac rupture) is much-discussed (the most-cited paper is Edwards, Gabel & Hosmer, *JAMA*, 1986) but **speculative** — a reconstruction, not a finding.
- **The day.** The **Synoptics** make the Last Supper a Passover meal, so Jesus dies the day after Passover begins; **John** places the death on the **Day of Preparation**, *as the Passover lambs are slaughtered* — aligning Jesus with the paschal lamb (John 1:29; 19:36). Most critical scholars judge John's dating theologically motivated; harmonizing proposals (two calendars) exist. The divergence is widely treated as genuine.
- **The year** (30 vs. 33 CE) remains unsettled, and some scholars hold precise dating is unreachable.
### Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted

*Christianity has never had a single doctrine of* how *the cross saves; it has several, and the traditions divide over which is central. These are reported, not adjudicated.*

- **Christus Victor / ransom** *(patristic; the Eastern emphasis).* On the cross Christ defeats sin, death, and the devil; the "ransom" variant (which Anselm criticized) pictured a price paid to free captive humanity. Revived in the West by Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931).
- **Satisfaction** *(Anselm, Cur Deus Homo, c. 1098).* Sin dishonors God; Christ's death restores the honor owed — an alternative to mere punishment.
- **Penal substitution** *(the Reformation, esp. Calvin).* A forensic development: Christ bears the *punishment* due to human sin, satisfying divine justice. Central to much evangelical and Reformed theology.
- **Moral influence** *(Abelard, 12th c.).* The cross supremely *demonstrates God's love*, moving the sinner to repentance — subjective rather than transactional.
  A deep fault line runs between the **Western** (juridical, substitutionary) and **Eastern Orthodox** (Christus Victor, *theosis*, less courtroom-shaped) frames. **Catholic** theology adds the **Mass** as a re-presentation — not a repetition — of the one sacrifice. Across all of them, the cross is the **center of Christian identity**: Paul resolves to know nothing "save Jesus Christ, and him crucified" (1 Cor 2:2) and calls "the message of the cross" the power of God (1 Cor 1:18). Many theologians now treat the models as complementary facets rather than rivals.

**How it has shifted.** Emphasis moved from the early **Christus Victor** picture, through Anselm's medieval **satisfaction** and the Reformation's **penal substitution**, to a modern period in which the substitutionary model is itself contested (some find it morally troubling) and the older *Christus Victor* and *moral influence* readings have been recovered.

### Allegorical reading (figural & typological senses)
*The literal sense is a Roman execution; the figural readings — long central to Christian thought — sit on top of it, each attributed and graded.*

- **The Passover lamb.** Christ as "our Passover, sacrificed for us" (1 Cor 5:7); John times the death to the slaughter of the lambs and cites "not a bone broken" (Exodus 12). *Grade:* a **typology invited by John's own text** — structurally central.
- **The Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53.** "He was wounded for our transgressions … by his stripes we are healed," read since Acts 8 as fulfilled in the cross. *Grade:* a foundational **Christian figural reading**; Jewish tradition reads the Servant as Israel — a defining interpretive battleground.
- **The binding of Isaac (the Aqedah).** The beloved son, the wood carried up the hill, the ram in his place — read by the Fathers as a foreshadowing of the cross. *Grade:* a **retrospective Christian typology**, not a literary borrowing in either direction.
- **The bronze serpent.** "As Moses lifted up the serpent … so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14, looking to Numbers 21). *Grade:* a **typology drawn inside the New Testament.**
### Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)
*Owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona.* Here the usual axis works differently from, say, Genesis. The **execution itself is not a literal/figurative question at all** — believers and secular historians alike treat it as a real event. The live question is **what it accomplished** (the atonement debate above) and **how to read the supernatural signs** — the darkness, the torn veil, Matthew's earthquake and risen saints. On those: conservative and traditionalist Christians read them as literal occurrences; many mainline and critical readers take them as theological symbolism (the torn veil = access to God opened; the darkness = cosmic judgment). The **Resurrection**, by contrast, is where the sharp literal/figurative and faith/history divide falls — and that belongs to its own entry.

### Parallel Accounts, Comparison & Harmonization
This *is* the prime four-account harmonization case in the Bible, which is why the entry harmonizes-with-divergences-flagged throughout §1. The honest summary: the **core is firmly shared** — execution at Golgotha under Pilate, between two others, the inscription, the casting of lots, the death, the burial by Joseph of Arimathea — while the **framing details and the words from the cross differ by Gospel**, each serving that evangelist's theology. The classic divergences: **who carries the cross** (Simon in the Synoptics; Jesus himself in John); **the wording of the inscription**; **whether both criminals mock or one repents** (Mark/Matthew vs. Luke); **the words from the cross** (Mark/Matthew's cry of abandonment vs. Luke's forgiveness vs. John's "it is finished"); **the cosmic signs** (Matthew's earthquake and risen saints are unique); **the spear and the unbroken bones** (John only); and **the day** (Synoptic Passover vs. Johannine Day of Preparation). The method is not to flatten these into one super-account but to let the four stand and read what each is doing.

### Older sources & cross-tradition parallels

**Within the Old Testament (the narrative's own scaffolding).** *(Ancient-World / Hebrew-Bible Historian.)* The passion is told *through* earlier scripture — **Psalm 22** (the opening cry; the divided garments; the mockery), **Psalm 31** (commending the spirit), **Psalm 69** (the gall and vinegar), **Isaiah 53** (the Suffering Servant), and **Zechariah 12:10** ("him whom they pierced"). *Relationship:* **deliberate intertextual reuse by the evangelists** — the open scholarly question (above) is how much is "prophecy historicized" versus "history remembered."

**Cousins (other living traditions).** *(Comparative-Religion / Islam Scholar.)* **Islam distinctively denies the crucifixion.** The Qur'an (**al-Nisāʾ 4:157–158**) is generally read to say of Jesus (ʿĪsā): "they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them" (*shubbiha lahum*); "rather, God raised him to Himself." The dominant classical reading is **substitutionist** — another was made to resemble Jesus and died in his place (variously identified). But Islamic exegesis (*tafsīr*) is genuinely **diverse**: Todd Lawson (*The Crucifixion and the Qurʾan*) shows readings ranging from outright denial to acceptance of the historical event, locating much of the denial in later commentary rather than the Qur'anic text itself; a minority "swoon" reading (the Ahmadiyya) holds Jesus survived. *Relationship:* an **independent theological tradition that explicitly contests** the Christian and historical accounts — not a borrowing or a parallel development. *Jewish tradition* does not accept the atonement theology of the cross; modern Jewish scholars (Vermes, Levine) have recovered Jesus' Jewishness and actively counter antisemitic readings.

### Psychoanalytic reading (the depth-psychology lens)
*One contested lens among many, applied to the story's images and to the figure in the text, not to claims about real history.*

- **The cry of dereliction and the absent father.** The moment "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" has drawn depth-psychology readings of abandonment and the rupture of the parent–child bond at the heart of the story — the experience of God-forsakenness given a voice. *Grade:* an **applied** reading.
- **Sacrifice and the scapegoat.** **René Girard's** mimetic theory (more anthropological than strictly psychoanalytic) reads the crucifixion as the great *exposure* of the scapegoat mechanism — the innocent victim killed to discharge a community's violence, but here narrated *from the victim's side* so that the mechanism is unmasked rather than endorsed. *Grade:* an influential **applied / interpretive** reading.
  *What to keep in view:* these are ways of reading the story's symbolism, set beside the historical and theological readings, not above them.

### Schisms, sects & ecclesial crises (what this reading split off)
*(Schism & Sectarian-Formation Historian.)* No church split over *whether* Jesus was crucified, but the **meaning** of the cross has drawn doctrinal lines. The **atonement models** (above) track real divisions — the **East–West** divergence between *Christus Victor*/*theosis* and the juridical West, and, within Protestantism, sharp modern disputes over **penal substitution** (defended as the heart of the gospel by conservative evangelicals; rejected by others as portraying a wrathful God). The **Catholic Mass as sacrifice** was a central target of the **Reformation** (Protestants rejecting any "re-sacrificing" of Christ as detracting from the once-for-all cross, Hebrews 9–10) — one of the load-bearing quarrels of the 16th-century split, though that is properly owned at the Eucharist/Last Supper and Hebrews entries.

### Connections
**Within the Passion arc:** *The Trials before the Sanhedrin and Pilate* (the "King of the Jews" charge and the Matt 27:25 verse arise there); *The Last Supper* (the meal the cross fulfills — "this is my body … my blood"); *Gethsemane & the Arrest*; and, forward, *The Resurrection* — the cross and empty tomb a single unit.
**Old-Testament intertexts read as fulfilled:** Psalm 22; Psalm 31; Psalm 69; Isaiah 53; Zechariah 12:10; Exodus 12 (the Passover lamb, "not a bone broken"); Numbers 21 (the lifted-up serpent, via John 3:14); and the **binding of Isaac** (Gen 22) as type.
**New-Testament theology:** Paul's "message of the cross" (1 Cor 1:18; 2:2); the once-for-all sacrifice of **Hebrews 9–10**; "Christ our Passover" (1 Cor 5:7).

### Cultural Afterlife (impact on the world)

- **Symbol:** the **cross** and the **crucifix** — the central symbol of Christianity and among the most recognized symbols on earth; the empty cross of most Protestantism vs. the *corpus*-bearing crucifix of Catholicism and Orthodoxy; the **sign of the cross** in worship.
- **Idiom & language:** "a cross to bear," "crucify him," "Calvary," "Golgotha," "INRI," "the stations of the cross," "the good thief."
- **Art:** with the Nativity, the most-depicted subject in Western art — Grünewald's **Isenheim Altarpiece** (painted for a plague hospital, Christ's body covered in the patients' sores), Velázquez, Rubens, the shift from the *Christus triumphans* to the suffering *Christus patiens*, Salvador **Dalí's** *Christ of Saint John of the Cross* (1951), and Marc **Chagall's** *White Crucifixion* (1938), which reclaims Jesus as an emblem of Jewish suffering amid Kristallnacht.
- **Music:** **Bach's** *St Matthew Passion* and *St John Passion*; **Handel's** *Messiah*; Stainer's *The Crucifixion*; Pergolesi's *Stabat Mater*; the spiritual **"Were You There When They Crucified My Lord."**
- **Film & stage:** the Passion plays; Pasolini's *The Gospel According to St. Matthew* (1964); Scorsese's *The Last Temptation of Christ* (1988); and Mel Gibson's *The Passion of the Christ* (2004) — the last a live case study in this passage's harm axis (below).
- **Devotion & place:** the **Stations of the Cross** / Via Dolorosa; **Good Friday**; the **Church of the Holy Sepulchre** as the traditional site. Across secular culture, "the cross" became shorthand for **redemptive or innocent suffering**.
- **Harmful afterlife** *(Reception-History Harm Historian).* This passage carries one of the gravest harm histories in the corpus, recorded here as documented misuse, framed and rejected. The harm does not arise from the crucifixion itself but from **who the passion narratives were read to blame**.
  **The deicide charge — "Christ-killers."** *Text.* The Gospels, written decades later amid conflict between the early Church and the synagogue and a wish not to antagonize Rome, **shift emphasis from the Roman authority that actually carried out this political execution onto the Jewish leadership** — and **John** repeatedly says "the Jews." The single most weaponized verse, **Matthew 27:24–25** (Pilate washing his hands; the crowd's "His blood be on us, and on our children"), sits in the *trial* scene (owned by the Trials entry) but radiates into the whole passion. *Reception.* These texts were read to brand the Jewish people **collectively and for all generations** as the murderers of God (*deicide*) — embedded in Holy Week preaching and in **Passion plays** (above all **Oberammergau**, which kept the "blood" line until deleting it in 2000). *Consequence.* The charge **scaffolded and then instrumentalized** nearly two millennia of Christian antisemitism — medieval blood libels, Holy Week and Easter pogroms, ghettos and expulsions — and fed the rhetoric that culminated in the **Shoah**. *(well-attested; this is the basis of the H4 score, with the heavier H5 carried by the Trials entry where the blood-verse sits.)* *Counter-tradition.* The historical-critical consensus is plain: **Jesus was executed by Roman authority as a political threat** ("King of the Jews"), crucifixion being a Roman penalty for sedition. And the charge has been **authoritatively repudiated**: the Second Vatican Council's **Nostra Aetate** (1965) declared that Christ's passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today," and that Jews are not "rejected or accursed by God"; major Protestant bodies issued parallel statements; and scholars such as **Amy-Jill Levine** work directly to disarm the antisemitic reading. *Status.* **Framed and rejected** — authoritatively by the Catholic Church and mainstream Protestantism, and discredited by historians — while persisting in extremist rhetoric, which is why it must be named, not passed over. *(The contested reception of Mel Gibson's 2004 film turns on exactly this axis.)*
