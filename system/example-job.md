## THE READING

### 1 · The text

*A plain translation made directly from the Hebrew. Job runs to forty-two chapters, far too long to set out whole, so this gives the load-bearing passages — the spine of the book — kept close to the wording so the hard choices stay visible. Where the Hebrew is genuinely uncertain (and in Job it often is), it follows one reading, and the next section opens up the rest.*

**The wager (1–2).** 1:1 There was a man in the land of Uz named Job — blameless and upright, who feared God and turned from evil. … 1:8 The LORD said to the Accuser, "Have you considered my servant Job? There is no one like him on earth." 1:9 The Accuser answered, "Does Job fear God for nothing? 1:10 Have you not put a hedge around him and all he has? 1:11 But stretch out your hand and touch all that he has — he will curse you to your face." …

1:21 [When his children, flocks, and servants are destroyed in a single day, Job tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground, and says:] "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I will return there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." …

2:7 So the Accuser went out and struck Job with painful sores from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head. 2:8 He took a piece of broken pottery to scrape himself, and sat among the ashes. 2:9 His wife said to him, "Are you still holding on to your integrity? Curse God, and die." 2:10 He said, "You speak as one of the foolish women would speak. Shall we accept good from God, and not accept harm?"

**The opening of the poem (3).** 3:3 [Job breaks a seven-day silence and curses the day he was born:] "Let the day perish on which I was born, and the night that said, 'A man is conceived.' … 3:11 Why did I not die at birth, come from the womb and expire?"

**The friends' creed.** 4:7 [Eliphaz:] "Think now: who, being innocent, ever perished? Where were the upright destroyed?" … 8:20 [Bildad:] "God will not reject a blameless man, nor take the hand of evildoers." — *the principle the whole book is testing: the good prosper, the wicked suffer, so suffering proves guilt.*

**Job's protest.** 9:22 [Job:] "It is all one; therefore I say he destroys both the blameless and the wicked. … 9:24 The earth is given into the hand of the wicked; he covers the faces of its judges — if it is not he, then who?" …

13:15 "Though he kill me, I will wait for him — yet I will argue my ways to his face." …

19:25 "But I know that my vindicator lives, and at the last he will stand upon the dust. 19:26 And after my skin has been struck off — yet from my flesh I will see God. 19:27 I myself will see him, my own eyes, and not another's. My heart faints within me." …

23:3 "If only I knew where to find him, that I might come even to his seat! 23:4 I would lay my case before him and fill my mouth with arguments."

**The voice from the storm (38–41).** 38:1 Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind: 38:2 "Who is this who darkens counsel with words without knowledge? … 38:4 Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding. … 38:7 when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" … 40:15 "Look now at Behemoth, which I made along with you; he eats grass like an ox." … 41:1 "Can you draw out Leviathan with a hook?"

**Job's answer (40; 42).** 40:4 "See, I am of small account; what can I answer you? I lay my hand on my mouth." … 42:5 "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you. 42:6 Therefore I recant, and I am comforted upon dust and ashes."

**The verdict and the end (42).** 42:7 The LORD said to Eliphaz, "My anger burns against you and your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has." … 42:10 And the LORD restored Job's fortunes and gave him twice as much as before … 42:12 a thousand more than the first.

### 2 · How the translations differ — and what's at stake

Job is one of the hardest books in the Hebrew Bible to translate. Its poetry is dense and old, its vocabulary studded with words that occur nowhere else, and in a few places the text is plainly damaged. At each hard place a translator must choose, and the choice quietly settles part of what the book means. The public-domain English versions you are likeliest to meet — the King James Version (1611), the American Standard Version (1901), the modern World English Bible — part ways here, and so do the ancient witnesses, above all the Greek Septuagint, which is about a sixth *shorter* than the Hebrew. Five places carry most of the weight.

- **1:6 — "the Accuser," not yet "Satan."** The figure who proposes the test is, in the Hebrew, *ha-satan* — "*the* accuser," with the definite article. That article matters: it is a title, not a name. He is a functionary in God's heavenly court, something like a roving prosecutor whose job is to test loyalty, and he acts only with God's leave. The King James prints "Satan" — capitalized, no article — which silently reads the *later* figure, the devil of the New Testament, back into a book that has no such character. Centuries of art show the Devil tormenting Job; the Hebrew shows a courtier doing his assigned work. (See *Text & Composition* for how this figure developed.)

- **2:9 — what Job's wife actually says.** The Hebrew has her tell Job, "*barek* God, and die." *Barek* means "bless" — but here it is the scribes' polite swap for its opposite. Ancient copyists found it unbearable to write "curse God" and substituted "bless," trusting readers to hear the reversal; the same euphemism appears earlier when the Accuser predicts Job will "bless" God to his face (1:11). So the literal page reads "bless," the meaning is "curse," and every English version (KJV included) rightly prints "curse God, and die." Worth knowing because it is the only line the most famous woman in the book is given, and the Septuagint, uneasy with her, expands it into a long, pitiable speech that the Hebrew never had.

- **13:15 — defiant faith, or no hope at all?** This is one of the most quoted lines in the book, and it hangs on a single tiny word. The written Hebrew text (the *kethiv*) has *loʾ* — "**not**" — yielding "He will kill me; **I have no hope**." The reading tradition the scribes preserved alongside it (the *qere*) has *lô* — "**in him**" — yielding "Though he kill me, **yet will I trust in him**," which is the King James wording and the source of the phrase's life as a banner of faith. The two are near-homophones, one letter apart, and they point in opposite directions: serene trust, or a man who expects to be destroyed and means to argue his case anyway. The translation above leans toward the second, harder reading; the tradition has overwhelmingly preferred the first.

- **19:25–27 — a resurrection, or a day in court?** "I know that my redeemer liveth" (KJV) is, for many English-speakers, the most familiar sentence in Job — partly because Handel set it in *Messiah*. The Christian tradition heard it as Job foreseeing his own bodily resurrection and the risen Christ: "in my flesh shall I see God." But the Hebrew here is among the most broken in the book, and almost every word is contested. *Goʾel* is not "redeemer" in a churchly sense; it is the **kinsman-avenger**, the relative obliged to take your side and get you justice — here, the advocate Job is sure will one day vindicate him. And the crucial phrase can be read "**from** my flesh" (while still alive) or "**apart from** my flesh" (after death), so the verse may promise vindication in this life or beyond it. The famous "redeemer liveth" reading is a real possibility built on an unstable text; modern translators flag the uncertainty rather than ringing the resurrection bell.

- **42:6 — does Job give in?** The whole meaning of the ending turns on Job's last six Hebrew words, and they are deeply ambiguous. The verb *emʾas* means "I reject / despise / retract," but it has **no object** — the King James supplies one, "I abhor *myself*," though the Hebrew names nothing. And the closing phrase, *ʿal-ʿapar wa-eper*, "concerning dust and ashes," can be read at least three ways: "I repent **in** dust and ashes" (Job grovels), or "I am consoled / I change my mind **about** dust and ashes" (Job is reconciled to the human condition), or even that Job *retracts his earlier retraction*. So the book can end with Job crushed into submission, or quietly satisfied, or still subtly resisting. Whichever English you are holding has already decided for you whether the protester finally caves.

### 3 · Plain-English retelling

In the land of Uz — somewhere east, on the edge of the known world — there lived a man named Job. *(The book never makes him an Israelite; he is a good man from nowhere in particular, which is part of the point. His story is meant to be everyone's.)* He was as good as a person could be: honest, generous, careful with God, blessed with a large family and great wealth. The story tells us this plainly so that what happens next can't be explained away.

The scene shifts to heaven, to a kind of royal court where God sits among his attendants. Among them is the Accuser — *(in Hebrew "the satan," a title, not a name: a member of the court whose role is to test, like a prosecutor probing for weakness)*. God points to Job with something like pride. The Accuser is unimpressed. *Of course he's faithful — look what you've given him. Take it away and watch him turn on you.* It is a wager about whether goodness can be real, or whether it is only ever paid for.

God lets the Accuser strip Job bare. In a single afternoon the messengers arrive one after another, each finishing the last one's sentence: raiders, fire, a great wind, the children all dead under a collapsed roof. Job tears his clothes and shaves his head — the old gestures of grief — and says the line that has been spoken at funerals ever since: he came into the world with nothing and will leave with nothing, and what was given was never his to keep. He does not curse God.

So the test deepens. Job is struck with oozing sores head to foot. He sits down in the ash-heap outside the town — *(where the refuse was burned; the lowest place there was)* — and scrapes at his skin with a shard of broken pot. His wife, who has buried the same ten children, tells him to stop pretending and be done with it: "Curse God, and die." He refuses her too.

Then three friends come to sit with him. For seven days and nights they say nothing at all — *(the one thing in the book they get unambiguously right)*. And then Job opens his mouth, and the prose stops and the poetry begins, and it does not let up for thirty-five chapters.

Job curses the day he was born. He does not ask to die so much as wish he had never lived. And his friends, scandalized, begin to argue. Their case is simple and was, in that world, common sense: God is just; the just are rewarded and the wicked punished; therefore if Job is suffering this much, Job must have done something to deserve it. Round after round they press it, more sharply each time —

> Who, being innocent, ever perished?
> God does not reject a blameless man.
> Confess what you've hidden, and be healed.

— and round after round Job throws it back. He knows he has done nothing to earn this, and he will not lie about it to make God look fair. He says the unsayable thing: that God destroys the innocent and the guilty alike, that the world is handed over to the wicked and no one is keeping score. He stops talking to his friends and starts talking past them, to God — demanding not comfort but a hearing, a day in court, a chance to lay out his case and be answered.

> If only I knew where to find him —
> I would fill my mouth with arguments.

A fourth man, young Elihu, breaks in near the end with a long speech nobody answers, insisting the elders have it wrong and that suffering can be God's way of teaching. And then, without warning, the thing Job demanded actually happens.

God answers — out of a storm. But not the answer anyone expected. God does not explain. God does not justify himself, or mention the wager, or tell Job he passed a test. Instead, for four chapters, God asks Job questions — vast, wheeling, unanswerable questions about the world Job never made:

*Where were you when I laid the earth's foundations? Have you walked in the deep? Do you know when the wild goats give birth, or send the lightning on its way? Can you bind the stars, feed the lion, leash the wild ox to your plow?*

*Look at Behemoth, the great beast I made beside you. Look at Leviathan, the dragon of the sea — can you put a hook through his jaw and lead him home like a pet?*

It is the strangest answer to a question of justice ever given: not a reason, but a tour of a creation that is enormous, beautiful, wild, and not arranged around human beings at all. The God who speaks is not the tidy bookkeeper the friends believed in.

And Job — who wanted his day in court — is somehow satisfied. Not argued down; *answered* by being addressed at all. He had known God by hearsay, he says; now he has seen him. And he stops. *(His final words are famously ambiguous: whether he caves, or melts, or makes a kind of peace "on dust and ashes," the Hebrew refuses to settle.)*

Then the poetry stops and the prose returns, and the ending turns the screw one more time. God is angry — not at Job, but at the three friends, "for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has." The men who defended God are in the wrong; the man who accused him is in the right. And Job is given everything back, doubled: new wealth, new flocks, seven more sons and three more daughters, a long old age. The book closes on a restoration so neat it almost seems to belong to a different, gentler story — which, scholars think, it once did.

### 4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)

Job is built to break a comfortable idea — one that is older than the Bible and is still everywhere today: that suffering is deserved. That if something terrible happens to you, you must, at some level, have had it coming. The friends are not villains; they are decent men saying the respectable thing, defending a moral universe where good behavior is safe and God can be trusted to keep the accounts. The book sets that belief up carefully and then drops a blameless man into ruin to watch it shatter.

What makes Job remarkable is that it lets the protester win the argument. Job refuses to confess to sins he didn't commit just to make the system hold together. He insists on two things at once that the friends say can't both be true — *I am innocent* and *I am suffering* — and he is willing to take that contradiction straight to God and demand an answer. At the end, God's own verdict is that Job, the accuser, spoke rightly, and the pious friends spoke wrongly. The book sides with honest anguish over tidy theology.

And then the answer, when it comes, is not an answer. God never tells Job why — never mentions the wager, never offers a reason. Instead God overwhelms him with the sheer scale and strangeness of creation: a world full of storms and sea-monsters and wild animals that have nothing to do with human merit. The point seems to be that the universe is not a courtroom with humanity at the center, and that the demand for the world to be *fair*, in the simple sense, may be a category mistake. What Job gets is not an explanation but an encounter — and the book treats that as enough, or nearly.

The two endings sit uneasily together on purpose. The poem refuses to explain suffering; the prose frame then hands everything back, doubled, as if to explain it after all. Most readers feel the seam. The book does not smooth it over, and neither should we.

### 5 · Why this matters

Job is the West's foundational text for one of the oldest questions there is: *why do bad things happen to good people?* Whenever that question gets asked seriously — after a disaster, a diagnosis, a death — you are standing in the room Job built. Knowing the book gives you the vocabulary the whole conversation runs on.

It also seeded a surprising amount of everyday speech, almost all of it from the patient saint of the prose frame rather than the raging poet inside. **"The patience of Job"** means superhuman endurance (the phrase actually comes from the New Testament's James 5:11, not from Job's own mouth — the poetic Job is famously *im*patient). **"Job's comforters"** are people whose attempts at comfort only make things worse — which is exactly what the three friends become. **"The skin of my teeth"** (Job 19:20) means a hair's-breadth escape, and gave Thornton Wilder his play title *The Skin of Our Teeth*. **"The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away"** is read at funerals across the English-speaking world. And **Leviathan** and **Behemoth** — the two monsters God parades in the whirlwind — became the standard names for any overwhelming power: Thomas Hobbes called his great book of political theory *Leviathan* (1651), making Job's sea-dragon the emblem of the all-powerful state, an image still in use whenever someone calls a government or corporation a "leviathan."

Its literary reach is just as deep. The wager in heaven that opens the book is the direct model for the "Prologue in Heaven" that opens Goethe's *Faust*, where God and Mephistopheles bet over a human soul the same way. Job stands behind Dostoevsky's furious arguments about innocent suffering in *The Brothers Karamazov*, behind Archibald MacLeish's Pulitzer-winning play *J.B.*, behind the Coen brothers' *A Serious Man*, behind the opening words of Terrence Malick's film *The Tree of Life* ("Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?"). William Blake's twenty-one engraved *Illustrations of the Book of Job* are a peak of English visual art, and Ralph Vaughan Williams turned them into a ballet score. Once you know Job, you start to recognize how much of how we argue about suffering — and how much great art about it — is still working through this one strange, unresolved book.

## BEHIND THE TEXT

### Setting & Context
**When (in the story):** a vague patriarchal age — Job's wealth is counted in livestock and servants, he offers his own sacrifices with no priesthood or Temple, and he lives an impossibly long time, all signals the author uses to set the tale "long ago," like a fable.
**When (scholarly):** the book is hard to date and almost certainly came together in stages. The folktale frame (the prose of chapters 1–2 and 42) may be old; the poetic core is usually placed in or after the Babylonian exile — roughly the 6th–4th centuries BCE — when the question of undeserved suffering pressed hardest on Israel.
**Where:** the land of **Uz**, unlocated and probably meant to be — somewhere east or southeast, in the orbit of Edom and the "people of the east." Job is pointedly *not* an Israelite; the book never mentions the covenant, the law, or Israel's history.
**Background a modern reader needs:** the book is arguing against a belief scholars call the **retribution principle** — the assumption, deeply built into the ancient world (and into much of Proverbs and Deuteronomy), that the righteous prosper and the wicked suffer, so that prosperity signals virtue and disaster signals guilt. Almost everything the friends say follows from it. The reader also needs the **heavenly court**: ancient texts often picture God surrounded by a council of subordinate divine beings, and "the satan" is one of them — a prosecutor, not the Devil. Mourning customs frame the story: tearing one's robe, shaving the head, sitting in **ashes**, the **potsherd** for scraping sores, and the friends' seven days of silence were all recognized rites of grief.
**Cast:** **Job** — a blameless, wealthy non-Israelite of Uz; the suffering righteous man. **The Accuser** (*ha-satan*) — a member of the heavenly court who proposes the test; a title, not the later Devil. **Job's wife** — survivor of the same losses; speaks one fierce, despairing line. **Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, Zophar the Naamathite** — the three friends who defend the retribution principle. **Elihu the Buzite** — a young latecomer who delivers a long speech (chs. 32–37) that many scholars judge a later addition. **YHWH** — who permits the test, then answers Job from the whirlwind.

### Text & Composition
**Key original-language terms.** *Ha-satan* — "the accuser / adversary," a court title with the definite article, **not** a proper name (the developed figure "Satan" the Devil is much later; see below). *Tam* — the word for Job, usually "blameless," carries a sense of being *whole, of one piece, with integrity*; the book's drama is whether such wholeness can survive being stripped of everything. *Goʾel* (19:25) — the **kinsman-redeemer / avenger**, the relative bound to take your side and secure your justice; rendering it "redeemer" in the Christian sense already tilts the verse (see The Reading §2). The divine names shift between sections: the prose frame uses **YHWH** freely, while the poetic dialogue mostly avoids it, preferring the older, more "universal" titles **El, Eloah, Shaddai** — a clue that frame and poem come from different hands.
**Textual notes & variants.** Three composition seams are widely recognized. (1) The **third cycle of speeches** (roughly chs. 24–27) is disordered: Bildad's third speech is oddly short, Zophar's third is missing entirely, and Job seems to voice arguments that belong to his opponents — as if pages were lost or reshuffled early. (2) The **Elihu speeches** (chs. 32–37) interrupt the flow: Elihu is not named in the prologue's cast or the epilogue's verdict, his Hebrew style differs, and the narrative reads smoothly if he is removed — so most critics treat his speeches as a later insertion. (3) The **Septuagint** of Job is about a sixth shorter than the Hebrew; the early Christian scholar Origen marked the missing material with asterisks. A first-century Aramaic **Targum of Job** was among the Dead Sea Scrolls (11QtgJob), showing the book was being translated early. (The reader-facing cruxes — *ha-satan*, 13:15, 19:25, 42:6 — are walked through in The Reading §2 and not repeated here.)
**Authorship & date (traditional):** the Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b–15a) records a debate, with one view assigning the book to **Moses** and others placing Job in various eras; the same passage preserves the striking opinion that Job never existed at all.
**Authorship & date (scholarly):** anonymous. The poem is usually dated to the **exilic or post-exilic period** (6th–4th c. BCE), with an older folktale frame reworked around it. That Job is named alongside Noah and **Danel** (an ancient Canaanite sage) as a paradigm of righteousness in **Ezekiel 14:14, 20** suggests "Job" was already a known legendary figure the author could borrow.

### Is it historical?
This is not a story historical inquiry can reach, and — unusually — the tradition itself says so. **What the text claims:** a man named Job in the land of Uz, struck by catastrophe and restored. But the book reads as a crafted **didactic tale**, not a chronicle: there is no datable setting, the land of Uz is unlocated, the numbers are schematic (7,000 sheep, 7 sons and 3 daughters, all doubled at the end), and thirty-five chapters of intricate poetry are obviously composed, not transcribed. **What evidence could show:** essentially nothing — there is no horizon to excavate, no named, locatable place, no individual who could leave a trace. **What scholars conclude:** Job is **wisdom literature** — a thought-experiment in narrative form, exploring the problem of innocent suffering, and belonging to a known ancient Near Eastern genre of "righteous sufferer" texts (see *Older sources*). Remarkably, the rabbinic tradition records the same verdict in its own idiom: the Talmud preserves the opinion (attributed to Resh Lakish, *Bava Batra* 15a) that **"Job never existed and never was; he is a parable"** — a rare case of a religious tradition openly reading its own scripture as fiction meant to teach.
**Grade — as a literal event:** not treated as history; the book makes no claim evidence can test, and the tradition itself flags it as a parable — closest to **A0, no testable claim**. **As a record of a real ancient debate about divine justice:** **well-attested** — the question, and the genre that wrestled with it, are firmly documented across the ancient Near East.

### Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted
**Jewish tradition:** the rabbis debated Job's date and even his existence (*Bava Batra* 14b–15a), and read him variously as a righteous Gentile, a prophet to the nations, and a foil in the argument over divine justice — some uneasy with his audacity toward God, others admiring it. **Maimonides** (*Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23) treated the book as a philosophical text in which each speaker voices a school of thought on providence, with Job's own view maturing across the book.
**Christian tradition:** the dominant early reading made Job the model of **patient endurance** (James 5:11, "the patience of Job"), and **Gregory the Great's** vast *Moralia in Job* (6th c.) turned the book into a comprehensive moral and allegorical system that shaped medieval reading for centuries. Job 19:25 ("I know that my redeemer liveth") was heard as a prophecy of resurrection and of Christ, and entered the burial liturgy and Handel's *Messiah*. Job's innocent suffering was read as a **type of Christ**. **Calvin** preached an influential sermon series on Job.
**Academic / historical-critical:** focuses on composition (frame vs. poem, the Elihu insertion, the scrambled third cycle) and reads the book as a deliberate **protest against and subversion of** the retribution theology found elsewhere in scripture — Job as the Bible arguing with itself.
**How the understanding has shifted:** the popular image has nearly reversed. For most of history Job was **"patient Job,"** the saint who bore everything without complaint — the man of the prose frame. Modern readers, attending to the poem, have rediscovered **"protesting Job,"** the man who rages, accuses God of injustice, and demands a hearing — and who is finally told he was *right*. The reading of the whirlwind speeches has shifted too: once heard as a satisfying answer, they are now often read as a magnificent **non-answer** — God changing the subject from justice to wonder.

### Allegorical reading (figural & typological senses)
*The literal sense is a tale about innocent suffering. The figural readings built on top of it are mostly Christian and philosophical.*

- **Gregory the Great's *Moralia* (Christian moral allegory).** Gregory read the whole book on multiple levels at once — Job as a type of Christ and of the suffering Church, the friends as heretics, the events as a map of the moral life. It is the most influential figural reading of Job ever produced, and it is a **system the reader builds**, not one the text demands. *Grade:* **allegory imposed (and elaborated) by later readers** — coherent and historically enormous, but going far beyond the plain text.
- **Job as a type of Christ.** The innocent sufferer, abandoned and vindicated, was read as prefiguring Christ; 19:25 was folded into this. *Grade:* **imposed**, though the suffering-righteous theme gives it a foothold.
- **Maimonides' philosophical allegory.** Each character stands for a position on providence (Job begins as something like an Aristotelian and is corrected). *Grade:* a **reading built as a framework**, attributed and influential.

### Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)
This axis behaves oddly for Job, and it's worth saying why. Even most readers who take the Bible very literally treat **Job the man** as possibly historical but **the book** as a crafted wisdom composition — because thirty-five chapters of dense, structured poetry plainly are not a courtroom transcript. So there is little of the sharp literal-vs-figurative fight that surrounds, say, the primeval history or the miracles; almost no doctrine hinges on whether Job existed, and the tradition itself (as above) entertained that he didn't. The genuinely contested reading here is not literal-vs-figurative but interpretive: **whether the friends' theology is endorsed.** It is not — God explicitly condemns it (42:7) — yet readers and preachers have repeatedly quoted the friends' confident lines *as if* they were the book's own teaching, which is the root of its one real harm history (below). *(This field is owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona, who refreshes any survey data at lock; story-specific historicity polling for Job is essentially nonexistent, so no figures are cited.)*

### Parallel Accounts, Comparison & Harmonization
**n/a** — Job is told only once. **Ezekiel 14:14, 20** (Job listed with Noah and Danel as paradigmatic righteous men) and **James 5:11** ("the patience of Job") are *allusions* that interpret the figure, not parallel accounts; they are covered under Connections.

### Older sources & cross-tradition parallels
**Antecedents (ancient Near East).** *(Ancient-World Historian.)* Job belongs to a recognized ANE genre — the **"righteous sufferer."** The closest cousins are Mesopotamian: ***Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*** ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom," late 2nd millennium BCE), in which a pious man is struck by disease, social ruin, and abandonment by his god, laments the injustice, and is finally restored; and the **Babylonian Theodicy** (c. 1000 BCE), a formal **dialogue** between a sufferer and a friend debating whether the gods are just — strikingly close to the *form* of Job's central debate. An older Sumerian poem, **"A Man and His God,"** works the same theme. *Relationship:* **shared genre / common ANE tradition, not direct borrowing** *(well-attested on the genre)* — these are the family Job was born into, not a text it copies. Job's distinctives stand out against them: a sufferer presented as **genuinely blameless** (the Mesopotamian sufferers assume some hidden fault), and a God who actually **appears and speaks** rather than remaining silent. A second, separate thread: the monster **Leviathan** of the whirlwind speech (41) is the Canaanite sea-dragon **Litan / Lotan** of the **Baal Cycle** from Ugarit, and **Behemoth** its land counterpart. *Relationship:* **inherited West Semitic imagery, repurposed** *(well-attested shared heritage)* — Israel drew its picture of primeval chaos-monsters from the Canaanite world around it and bent it to its own God.

**Cousins (other living traditions).** *(Comparative-Religion / Islam Scholar.)* Job — **Ayyūb** — is a revered prophet in Islam, named in the **Qur'an** at **21:83–84** (al-Anbiyāʾ) and **38:41–44** (Ṣād). The Qur'anic Ayyūb is, above all, the model of **ṣabr** (patient endurance): afflicted by Satan with hardship and pain, he turns to God in prayer rather than protest, and God restores him — at 38:42 a cool spring gushes up when he strikes the ground with his foot, to wash and drink. A famous detail at **38:44** has God tell him to "take in your hand a bundle [of grass or twigs] and strike with it," which the commentary tradition explains as a merciful way for Ayyūb to keep an oath he had sworn to beat his wife, without truly harming her. *Relationship:* the **same figure**, in **shared tradition** *(well-attested)* — but with a pointed difference of emphasis. The Qur'an has **no long debate, no friends, no accusation of God**; Ayyūb is patient, not a protester. The irony is that the "patience of Job" the West inherited from the New Testament and the prose frame is far closer to the Qur'anic Ayyūb than to the biblical poem's furious, arguing Job.

**In later Islamic tradition.** *(Comparative-Religion / Islam Scholar.)* The *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* ("stories of the prophets") literature elaborates Ayyūb's trial — the loss of family and wealth, years of disease and abandonment, and his exemplary endurance — making him the proverbial pattern of steadfastness under affliction. *Relationship:* **shared tradition** developed around the common figure *(lightly attested in the canonical hadith; mainly in the qiṣaṣ and tafsīr)*.

### Psychoanalytic reading (the depth-psychology lens)
*One contested lens among many, and in Job's case dominated by a single famous text. These are readings of the book's figures and drama, not of any real person.*

- **Jung: *Answer to Job* (1952).** Carl Jung's late, deliberately provocative book is the landmark psychoanalytic reading. Jung took the heavenly wager seriously as a **drama inside the divine psyche**: a God who has a dark, unconscious side (figured by "the satan") and who, in tormenting a blameless man, is morally **outmatched by his own creature** — Job, who behaves better than the deity testing him. Jung read the whole arc as pressure building toward God's need to "become man." *Grade:* **imposed** — a bold theological-psychological construction the text does not ask for — but historically and culturally important, and influential on later readers of the book.
- **The Accuser as the shadow.** In broadly Jungian terms the satan figure is read as the **adversarial, shadow aspect** within the heavenly court — the necessary opponent whose testing forces something into the open. *Grade:* **plausible** as a way of seeing the court scene.

*What to keep in view:* psychoanalysis is itself contested as science, and Jung's *Answer to Job* is contested even within depth psychology as much theology as analysis. It is offered here as a way the book has been read — set beside the others, not above them. (Jung's cultural influence is handed to the Reception scholar, #7.)

### Connections
**Echoed / cited elsewhere:** **Ezekiel 14:14, 20** names Job (with Noah and Danel) as a paradigm of the righteous; **James 5:11** gives us "the patience of Job"; **1 Corinthians 3:19** quotes Job 5:13 ("He catches the wise in their craftiness"); **Romans 11:35** echoes Job 41:11 ("Who has given to me, that I should repay?"). **Themes & motifs:** the suffering of the righteous; **theodicy** (the problem of divine justice); the limits of human wisdom; the wildness and otherness of creation; the inadequacy of easy piety; honest protest as a form of faith.

### Cultural Afterlife (impact on the world)
- **Art:** **William Blake's *Illustrations of the Book of Job*** (21 engravings, 1826) are a monument of English art and the most famous visual treatment; **Georges de La Tour's** *Job Mocked by His Wife* is a Baroque high point; the suffering Job on his dunghill is a recurring subject from medieval manuscripts onward.
- **Literature:** **Goethe's *Faust*** opens with a "Prologue in Heaven" modeled directly on Job's wager; **Archibald MacLeish's *J.B.*** (1958) is a verse-drama retelling that won the Pulitzer Prize; **Robert Frost's *A Masque of Reason*** (1945) imagines Job and God meeting again; **Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov*** turns on the problem of innocent suffering and quotes Job directly (the elder Zosima loves the book); **Joseph Roth's *Job*** (1930) and **H. G. Wells's *The Undying Fire*** are modern transpositions; Job's atmosphere hangs over **Kafka**.
- **Music:** **Ralph Vaughan Williams**, *Job: A Masque for Dancing* (1930), built on Blake's images; **Hubert Parry's** oratorio *Job* (1892); Job 19:25, "I know that my redeemer liveth," set as a soprano aria in **Handel's *Messiah*** (1741) — there read as a resurrection text.
- **Film, TV & stage:** the **Coen brothers' *A Serious Man*** (2009) is a modern Job set in 1960s suburbia; **Terrence Malick's *The Tree of Life*** (2011) opens on screen with Job 38:4; MacLeish's *J.B.* and Neil Simon's comic *God's Favorite* (1974) brought Job to Broadway.
- **Language & idiom:** **"the patience of Job"** (endurance); **"Job's comforters"** (would-be comforters who deepen the wound); **"the skin of my teeth"** (a hair's-breadth escape — Job 19:20, and Thornton Wilder's play *The Skin of Our Teeth*); **"the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away"** (a funeral commonplace); **"naked I came… naked I shall return."**
- **Politics, law & social movements:** **Thomas Hobbes** named his foundational work of political theory ***Leviathan*** (1651) after the sea-monster of Job 41, making Job's beast the lasting emblem of the overwhelming power of the state; he reused **Behemoth** as the title of his book on the English Civil War, and Franz Neumann borrowed *Behemoth* (1942) for his study of Nazi Germany. "A leviathan" still means any vast, unaccountable power.
- **Harmful afterlife** *(Reception-History Harm Historian).* Job is one of scripture's *least* weaponized books — strikingly, because the book's whole purpose is to refute the very idea most open to misuse. One documented misuse-pattern exists, and it works by quoting the wrong characters.

  **The friends' theology turned on the afflicted.**
  *Text.* Job 4–37 puts the **retribution principle** — suffering proves guilt; misfortune is deserved punishment — in the mouths of the **three friends**, whom God then explicitly **condemns**: "you have not spoken of me what is right" (42:7). The book exists to reject this reading. *Reception.* Readers and preachers have nonetheless lifted the friends' confident lines (and the broader Deuteronomic "the righteous prosper" theology) out of context and used them to **blame the sufferer** — telling the sick, the disabled, the poor, and the bereaved that their affliction reveals hidden sin or weak faith; the modern "prosperity gospel" recycles the same logic (suffering as a failure of faith). *Consequence.* Documented **pastoral harm** — shame, isolation, and spiritual abuse heaped on people already suffering; **amplified by** older retribution theology rather than originated by Job. The scope is limited and personal, not tied to mass violence or law. *(well-attested as a pattern; the harm is real but bounded)* *Counter-tradition.* **The book of Job is itself the counter-text** — it was written to demolish exactly this argument, and it ends by vindicating the protester and rebuking the system's defenders. *Status.* A **misreading against the book's own explicit verdict; framed and rejected.**

  A second, minor strand: **Job's wife** ("Curse God, and die," 2:9) drew a long tradition of harsh reading — she was cast as an **instrument of Satan**, even as the one affliction the Accuser deliberately left Job alive in order to use against him — a hard verdict on a woman who had buried the very same ten children. Modern readers push back: the Septuagint already gives her a long, pitiable speech the Hebrew lacks, and scholars such as Ilana Pardes and Carol Newsom read her as a **fellow victim** of the test rather than its agent.
- **Popular culture:** **Leviathan** and **Behemoth** saturate fantasy, science fiction, and video games as the default names for colossal monsters and overwhelming forces; "a Job-like trial" is shorthand for relentless, undeserved misfortune.
