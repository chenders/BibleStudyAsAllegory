translations differ, a plain-English retelling, what it means, and why it matters,

## THE READING

### 1 · The text
*One clear, accurate English translation made directly from the original language
(Hebrew / Aramaic / Greek), kept close to the wording so the hard choices stay
visible. Where the original is genuinely uncertain, follow one reading here and open
up the rest in section 2. Open with a one-line italic lead note saying what this
translation is. Editorial translation — never paste an in-copyright version; check
against scholarly translations and lexica.*

### 2 · How the translations differ — and what's at stake
*The cruxes a newcomer would never catch on their own: the handful of places where
translations diverge and the meaning turns on the choice. For each, name the versions
(KJV / ASV / WEB and the ancient witnesses — Septuagint, Samaritan, Vulgate, Targums,
Syriac), say plainly what each does, and explain what is at stake. This replaces a
second full translation: rather than printing the KJV in parallel, show the reader
exactly where and why the famous wordings part company. Coordinate the textual detail
with* Text & Composition *in Behind the Text so the two don't duplicate. Owned by the
Translation & Languages persona (#2) with the Narrative Writer (#9); checked by
Standards & Integrity (#10). If the passage has no real translation cruxes, keep this
short or mark n/a.*

### 3 · Plain-English retelling
*The story told freshly in easy modern words — vivid, accessible to a curious
teen or adult. Explain any reference a general reader wouldn't know (a custom, a
place-name pun, an idiom) right where it comes up, in a short aside.*

### 4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)
*In simple language: what the passage is getting at — its point and main themes —
for a general reader. This is the accessible interpretation; the deeper, sourced,
multi-tradition version lives in "Behind the Text."*

### 5 · Why this matters
*The learner's payoff — written for a curious newcomer with no church background.
A plain, direct answer to "so what": why this passage is worth knowing, and where
you will actually run into it — in everyday language and idiom, law and politics,
art and music, and the inherited assumptions of the surrounding culture. What does
knowing this let you see that you'd otherwise miss? Honest and concrete, never
preachy; this is not an argument that the text is true, but an account of why it
has mattered. (Owned by the Narrative Writer.)*

### Is it historical?
*What history, archaeology, and science actually say about whether the events
happened, were embellished, or are symbolic/theological. Keep three things
separate: (a) what the text itself claims, (b) what physical, archaeological, or
scientific evidence shows, (c) what mainstream scholars conclude. Grade the
evidence plainly — **well-attested · contested · rejected** — and name the real
debate where one exists. For primeval or legendary material, say directly that it
isn't treated as datable history, and why that isn't the same as saying it's
"false." Owned by the Ancient-World Historian with the Historical-Critical scholar.*

### Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted
*The deep version of section 4: how Jewish, Catholic/Orthodox/Protestant,
academic, and other traditions have read it (attributed to each), and how the
dominant understanding has changed over time. Name the real debates.*

### Allegorical reading (figural & typological senses)
*Optional — when the text carries a significant allegorical tradition or invites
one. What the story has been taken to mean beyond its literal sense: Christian
typology and the fourfold sense (literal · allegorical · moral · anagogical), Jewish
midrash and PaRDeS, Islamic ta'wil, and the story's life as literary allegory. State
the literal sense first, then give each figural reading attributed to its tradition
or reader ("Origen read the Red Sea crossing as…", "in the fourfold sense Jerusalem
signifies…"). Grade it — **the text invites allegory · allegory imposed by later
readers** — and keep it distinct from the broad reading-history in *Interpretation
across traditions*. Flag where typology devalued the older scripture as a mere shadow
(supersessionism) and hand that to the Harm Historian (#11). Owned by the Allegorical
& Figural Reader (#17). Otherwise: n/a.*

### Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)
*Owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona. First say whether the axis even
applies (parables/poetry are figurative by design; core creedal events are taken
as literal-historical by nearly all believers; the sharpest splits are in the
primeval history, miracles, and a few narratives). Then give the denominational
tendencies (which groups lean literal vs. figurative) and any current, cited
survey data — keeping the whole-Bible numbers distinct from this-story belief.*

### Parallel Accounts, Comparison & Harmonization
*Only when the event is told 2+ times. Where the versions are · what's shared ·
what conflicts · how harmonizers reconciled it · how source critics explain it.
Otherwise: n/a (note any mere allusions under Connections instead).*

### Older sources & cross-tradition parallels
*The comparative-religion lens — how this story relates to, contrasts with, and
borrows from material outside the Bible. Two kinds: (a) **antecedents** — older
ancient Near Eastern (or other) sources the text reworks, e.g. the Mesopotamian
flood epics behind Noah; (b) **cousins** — how the same story appears in other
living traditions, above all the Qur'an. For each, say what is shared, what
differs, and the scholarly read on the relationship — using graded language
(**direct borrowing · shared common tradition · independent parallel ·
coincidence**), never asserting dependence as fact where scholars debate it.
Owned by the Ancient-World Historian (ANE antecedents) and the Comparative-Religion
specialist (other faiths). Otherwise: n/a.*

### Psychoanalytic reading (the depth-psychology lens)
*Optional — only when the story rewards it. How the narrative reads through the
depth-psychology traditions (Freud, Jung, and their heirs): its drives, symbols,
family structure, and archetypes. Attribute every reading to a school or named
critic ("In a Freudian reading…", "Jung took Job to be…"); keep psychoanalysis's
own contested scientific standing in view; and read the figures as figures in a
story, never as clinical diagnoses of real or historical people. Trace an archetype
across the corpus (Bible, Qur'an, ANE) where it recurs. Grade the reading —
**invited by the text · plausible · imposed** — and hand documented harm to the
Harm Historian (#11) and cultural influence to the Reception scholar (#7). Owned by
the Psychoanalytic & Depth-Psychology Critic (#16). Otherwise: n/a.*
