## THE READING

### 1 · Faithful translation
*An accurate, fairly literal English translation made from the Hebrew/Greek —
modern and readable, close to what the text actually says. (Editorial
translation; in production, check against standard scholarly translations and
lexica.)*

### 2 · King James Version
*The exact KJV text (public domain — pull from a verified source). The familiar
traditional wording, worth setting beside the faithful translation because the
KJV's English is older and its underlying manuscripts differ in places.*

### 3 · Plain-English retelling
*The story told freshly in easy modern words — vivid, accessible to a curious
teen or adult. Explain any reference a general reader wouldn't know (a custom, a
place-name pun, an idiom) right where it comes up, in a short aside.*

### 4 · What it means (plain-English interpretation)
*In simple language: what the passage is getting at — its point and main themes —
for a general reader. This is the accessible interpretation; the deeper, sourced,
multi-tradition version lives in "Behind the Text."*

### 5 · Why this matters
*The learner's payoff — written for a curious newcomer with no church background.
A plain, direct answer to "so what": why this passage is worth knowing, and where
you will actually run into it — in everyday language and idiom, law and politics,
art and music, and the inherited assumptions of the surrounding culture. What does
knowing this let you see that you'd otherwise miss? Honest and concrete, never
preachy; this is not an argument that the text is true, but an account of why it
has mattered. (Owned by the Narrative Writer.)*

### Is it historical?
*What history, archaeology, and science actually say about whether the events
happened, were embellished, or are symbolic/theological. Keep three things
separate: (a) what the text itself claims, (b) what physical, archaeological, or
scientific evidence shows, (c) what mainstream scholars conclude. Grade the
evidence plainly — **well-attested · contested · rejected** — and name the real
debate where one exists. For primeval or legendary material, say directly that it
isn't treated as datable history, and why that isn't the same as saying it's
"false." Owned by the Ancient-World Historian with the Historical-Critical scholar.*

### Interpretation across traditions & how it has shifted

### Literal or figurative? (who reads it which way)
*Owned by the Sociologist of Religion persona. First say whether the axis even
applies (parables/poetry are figurative by design; core creedal events are taken
as literal-historical by nearly all believers; the sharpest splits are in the
primeval history, miracles, and a few narratives). Then give the denominational
tendencies (which groups lean literal vs. figurative) and any current, cited
survey data — keeping the whole-Bible numbers distinct from this-story belief.*

### Parallel Accounts, Comparison & Harmonization

### Older sources & cross-tradition parallels
*The comparative-religion lens — how this story relates to, contrasts with, and
borrows from material outside the Bible. Two kinds: (a) **antecedents** — older
ancient Near Eastern (or other) sources the text reworks, e.g. the Mesopotamian
flood epics behind Noah; (b) **cousins** — how the same story appears in other
living traditions, above all the Qur'an. For each, say what is shared, what
differs, and the scholarly read on the relationship — using graded language
(**direct borrowing · shared common tradition · independent parallel ·
coincidence**), never asserting dependence as fact where scholars debate it.
Owned by the Ancient-World Historian (ANE antecedents) and the Comparative-Religion
specialist (other faiths). Otherwise: n/a.*
