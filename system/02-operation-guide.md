# Operation Guide

This is the methodology for the project: how we cut the Bible into units, what
each entry contains, how we calibrate before scaling, and the standing decisions
that keep everything consistent. Read this once; refer back to it when a new kind
of passage breaks the usual mold.

## Who this is for (and what that changes)

This is a **teaching tool for a curious newcomer** — someone not religious, with
no church background, who has never read the Bible and wants to understand it as
cultural and historical inheritance. That single fact drives several standing
choices:

- **Assume no prior knowledge.** Explain every name, custom, and reference a
  newcomer wouldn't know. Never assume familiarity with the text or with
  Christianity.
- **Every entry earns its keep with a "why this matters."** Reading layer 5 gives
  the learner's payoff — where the passage surfaces in language, law, art, and
  politics — concretely and without preaching.
- **Historicity is graded, not asserted.** In *Is it historical?*, separate (a)
  what the text claims, (b) what the physical/archaeological evidence shows, and
  (c) what scholars conclude, and label the evidence **well-attested · contested ·
  rejected**. "Not datable history" is not the same as "false," and we say so.
- **Comparison is graded too.** In *Older sources & cross-tradition parallels*,
  describe how the story relates to older Near Eastern sources and to other faiths
  (especially the Qur'an) using **borrowing · shared tradition · independent
  parallel · coincidence** — never asserting dependence where scholars debate it.
- **The long horizon is comparative.** The Bible comes first, but the comparative
  habit is deliberate groundwork for eventually treating other foundational texts
  (the Qur'an and others) the same way.

## 1. The core idea

For each unit of the Bible we produce a single **entry** with two halves:

- **A modern retelling** — the story told freshly in clear, vivid present-day
  English, faithful to the events and the original setting.
- **Structured metadata** — context, composition, whether it's historical,
  interpretation across traditions, how the meaning has shifted over time, older
  sources and cross-tradition parallels, cultural influence, and — as the reader's
  payoff — why it matters.
  Think of the result as a cross between a modern paraphrase (like *The Message* or
  *The Bible Project* in spirit) and the apparatus of a good study Bible or the
  *Encyclopedia of the Bible and Its Reception* — but in one consistent, readable
  format.

### Two layers: entries and the reference catalogue
The project runs on two interlocking layers. **This entry layer** produces the deep,
per-pericope entries (template + the review panel). Underneath sits a **reference /
index layer** — the `R` files — that catalogues and scores the whole field:

- **`R0`** — the shared **scoring rubric** (five axes, each 0–5: **I**nfluence ·
  **C**ontestedness · **P**opularity · **H**arm · **A**ttestation) and the
  **controlled keyword taxonomy** (genre, theme, figures, places, cross-corpus links).
- **`R1`** — the **master catalogue**: the whole Protestant 66, unit by unit, each
  with a one-line description, links, keywords, and an `I·C·P·H·A` score. This is the
  authoritative index; the Story Index (`06`) is the *production plan* that points to it.
- **`R2` Torah (Jewish framing)** · **`R3` Quran** · **`R4` Hadith** ·
  **`R5` ANE & related sources** — the cross-corpus references the comparative and
  historical fields draw on (the working shelves for personas #4, #5, #13).
  Every deep entry carries the same **score line and taxonomy tags** as its `R1` stub,
  so the two layers stay linked and ~1,000 units cross-search consistently.

### How we prioritize what to draft
Use the `R0` scores to choose and frame work, not to rank importance crudely:
- **Draft order** follows narrative sequence, but within that we reach first for the
  high **I + P** units (cultural weight — the ones a newcomer most needs).
- **High H** is a flag to slow down and frame carefully (the harm block, owned by #11).
- **A** signals how much the *Is it historical?* field will carry — A0/A1 units lean
  on the symbolic/etiological reading; A3–A5 units invite real evidence discussion.

## 2. How to break up the Bible

The Bible is not one genre, so "story" can't be the only unit. We divide it into
**pericopes** — the scholarly term for a self-contained passage with its own
integrity. A pericope is usually obvious: a scene, an episode, a single parable,
a single psalm, one law collection, one prophetic oracle, one argument in a
letter.

**Default granularity:** one coherent episode = one entry. Cain and Abel
(Gen 4:1–16) is one unit; the genealogy that follows (4:17–26) is a separate,
lighter unit. Don't split a scene that belongs together; don't merge two scenes
just because they're adjacent.

**Unit types and how the entry adapts:**

| Material | Unit | How the "retelling" works |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative (Genesis, Exodus, Judges, Samuel–Kings, Gospels, Acts) | Episode / scene | Straight modern retelling. The default case. |
| Parables & sayings (much of the Gospels) | One parable or saying-cluster | Retell the parable; metadata carries the teaching context and interpretations. |
| Law (much of Exodus–Deuteronomy) | One law or law collection | "Retelling" becomes a plain-language *rendering* of what the law required and why; metadata carries ancient context and afterlife. |
| Poetry & wisdom (Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs) | One psalm / poem / discourse | Don't force a narrative. Render the poem in modern English and annotate; metadata does the heavy lifting. |
| Prophets | One oracle or narrated episode | Mixed: retell the narrative bits, render and gloss the oracles. |
| Epistles (NT letters) | One argument-section | "Retelling" becomes a plain-language paraphrase of the argument; metadata carries occasion, audience, and reception. |
| Apocalyptic (Daniel visions, Revelation) | One vision | Retell the vision vividly; metadata unpacks the imagery and its many readings. |

When a passage doesn't fit the schema cleanly, note it and adapt rather than
forcing it — those edge cases are exactly what the calibration phase exists to
surface.

**Parallel passages.** Some stories exist in multiple versions (the four Gospel
accounts of the crucifixion; Synoptic parallels; Kings vs Chronicles). Default:
one primary entry that notes the parallels and their differences, rather than
near-duplicate entries. Decide the lead text per case.

## 3. Standing decisions (the conventions that keep us consistent)

These are the defaults. Any of them can be changed during calibration — but once
changed, change them here so every future entry follows suit.

**3.1 What "modern version" means.**
Default = a *faithful modern-prose retelling*: contemporary, vivid, readable
English that keeps the original setting, characters, and events. We are **not**
transposing the stories into a present-day world (no "Cain and Abel as rival
startup founders"). If you ever want that, it becomes a clearly-labeled optional
*transposition* variant, separate from the canonical retelling. → confirm in
calibration.

**3.2 Canon scope.**
Start with the **Protestant 66-book canon** (most familiar, smallest, simplest).
Build in hooks to extend later to the Catholic/Orthodox deuterocanonical books
(Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Maccabees, etc.) and to note where the Jewish
Tanakh orders or divides things differently. → confirm in calibration.

**3.3 Translation & copyright (important).**
- The **retelling is always original prose.** That is what keeps the whole
  project free of translation copyright.
- When you need to **quote** scripture verbatim in the metadata, use only
  **public-domain** translations: the **World English Bible (WEB)** as the
  modern-readable default, the **KJV** for traditional flavor, **ASV (1901)** if
  useful. Keep quotes short.
- **Never paste** in-copyright translations: NIV, ESV, NLT, NASB, CSB, NRSV,
  *The Message*, etc. Paraphrase their sense in your own words instead.
- Original-language words (Hebrew/Greek) and their transliterations are fine and
  often valuable.
  **3.4 Neutrality & multiple traditions.**
  Treat the Bible as a literary, historical, and cultural work and report how it
  has been read, without adjudicating truth claims. For each interpretation, name
  the tradition or school ("In rabbinic tradition…", "Augustine read this as…",
  "Historical critics generally date…"). Always separate four layers:
1. what the text says,
2. traditional faith interpretation(s),
3. academic / historical-critical scholarship,
4. later reception and cultural afterlife.
   **3.5 Sensitive reception.**
   Some passages carry harmful interpretive histories (the "curse/mark of Cain"
   misused to justify racism and slavery; texts misused against Jewish people, women,
   or LGBTQ+ people). Include these accurately as **reception history**, clearly
   framed as misuse with no basis in the text, never endorsed and never given the
   how-to detail of the harmful argument.

**3.6 Tone.**
Warm, clear, confident, never preachy and never sneering. We respect believers
and skeptics alike. Wonder is welcome; proselytizing and debunking are not.

**3.7 Output & rendering (the reading page).**
Every finished entry is rendered to a single self-contained, dark, screen-native
**HTML reading page** — not a PDF, not ad-hoc styling — by running
`render-entry.py ENTRY.md`. The page's look and behaviour (palette, typography, the
five-axis score with its per-axis tooltips, the "Click here to see the table of
contents" disclosure, the gutter section-nav, the mobile and accessibility floor, and
the embedded subset fonts) are fixed by the **Rendering & Build Spec**, owned by the
Visual & Front-End Design Reviewer (#15); explanatory charts, maps, and diagrams
inside a page are built by the Explanatory Graphics & Motion Designer (#18). The
renderer is structure-driven, so an entry only renders correctly if it follows the
template's headings and the Score-line format. If the look ever needs to change,
change the renderer and the spec **together** (and re-render the worked example to
confirm it still matches) — never restyle one page by hand, or the stories drift
apart. → confirm in calibration.

## 4. The calibration plan (start small, then scale)

We deliberately tune the format on familiar stories before committing to ~1,000
entries.

**Phase 0 — Setup (now).** Lock the template, style guide, metadata fields, and
the standing decisions above. *(These draft files are Phase 0.)*

**Phase 1 — Pilot: Cain and Abel.** One short, familiar story taken all the way
to a finished entry (see the worked example). React to it: right length? right
depth per field? right voice? Adjust the template.

**Phase 2 — Calibration set.** A small, deliberately *diverse* set chosen to
stress-test the schema across genres and difficulty. Suggested set:

1. **Creation — Genesis 1** (structured/poetic; the Gen 1 vs Gen 2 two-accounts
   problem)
2. **Cain and Abel — Genesis 4** *(the pilot)*
3. **Noah's Flood — Genesis 6–9** (long; composite J/P sources; ancient Near
   Eastern parallels like Gilgamesh)
4. **Binding of Isaac — Genesis 22** (ethically thorny; vast reception; Jewish,
   Christian, and Islamic readings)
5. **The Exodus & Passover — Exodus 12–14** (narrative + ritual; enormous
   political/cultural afterlife)
6. **David and Goliath — 1 Samuel 17** (the underdog archetype; idiom)
7. **Psalm 23** (tests non-narrative poetry — how do you "retell" a psalm?)
8. **The Prodigal Son — Luke 15:11–32** (parable genre; NT)
9. **The Crucifixion — Gospels** (four parallel accounts; harmonization;
   theological weight)
10. *(optional)* **1 Corinthians 13** or a passage from **Revelation** (tests
    epistle / apocalyptic — the hardest fits)
    If the schema survives all of these, it will survive the rest. Each one that
    *doesn't* fit teaches us a schema fix.

**Phase 3 — Lock the spec & build the full index.** Freeze the template. Expand
`Story Index` into the complete list of units for the whole canon, book by book.

**Phase 4 — Production at scale.** Work book by book. Suggested order: Genesis →
the rest of the Torah → the Gospels (high familiarity, high payoff) → historical
books → prophets → wisdom/poetry → epistles → Revelation. Batch, then QA.

**Ongoing — Maintenance.** Add cross-links between related entries; keep a running
list of recurring characters, places, and motifs so they're described
consistently everywhere.

## 5. Quality checklist (run before marking an entry "reviewed")

- [ ] Reference and unit boundaries are correct and stated.
- [ ] Retelling is original prose, faithful to the events, in the project voice.
- [ ] No copyrighted translation is quoted; any quotes are public-domain and short.
- [ ] Every interpretation is attributed to a tradition or school.
- [ ] Real scholarly debates and textual problems are named, not hidden.
- [ ] Cultural-influence claims are accurate and specific (named works, idioms),
  not vague ("inspired many artists").
- [ ] Any harmful reception is framed clearly as misuse.
- [ ] Cross-references and "related entries" are filled.
- [ ] Sources / further reading included.
- [ ] Status line + date present.
- [ ] Renders cleanly to the standard reading page via `render-entry.py` (see `Rendering & Build Spec`).

## 6. Reference models (worth borrowing from)

- **The Bible Project** — clear, accessible explanation of structure and themes.
- ***The Message* (Eugene Peterson)** — a benchmark for bold modern paraphrase
  (in copyright — study the *approach*, don't copy text).
- **Study Bible apparatus** (e.g., the structure of study notes, intros,
  cross-references) — a model for the metadata layout.
- **Blackwell Bible Commentaries** — explicitly organized around *reception
  history*, our model for the "how understanding has shifted" and "cultural
  influence" fields.
- **Encyclopedia of the Bible and Its Reception (EBR)** — the gold standard for
  tracing a text's afterlife across art, literature, and culture.
- **Wikipedia's better Bible articles** — a usable model for neutral,
  multi-tradition summary and for "in popular culture" sections.
