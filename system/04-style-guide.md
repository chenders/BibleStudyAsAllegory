# Style Guide

This governs the **retelling** — the modern-prose half of each entry. Consistency
of voice across hundreds of entries is the hardest thing to maintain, so keep
this close while writing.

---

## Voice in one line

Tell it the way a gifted modern novelist would tell an old, true-feeling story:
clear, grounded, unhurried, with an eye for the human moment — never archaic,
never preachy, never winking at the reader.

**Write for a curious newcomer** with no church background: explain references in
a short aside the moment they appear, and assume nothing. The new *Why this
matters* layer (5) uses this same plain voice — concrete about where the story
shows up in the culture, honest, and never an argument that it's true.

## Defaults

- **Setting:** keep the original world. Modern *language*, ancient *setting*.
  Don't transpose to the present day (that's a separate optional variant).
- **Person & tense:** third person, past tense, unless a passage's own form calls
  for otherwise.
- **Length:** ~300–800 words for a typical episode. Shorter for a small scene;
  longer for a sweeping one. Don't bloat a quiet passage to hit a count.
- **Reading level:** accessible to a curious adult or older teen. Short, strong
  sentences. Concrete nouns and verbs over abstraction.

## Do

- **Show the human stakes** — fear, envy, grief, hope — through action and image,
  not commentary.
- **Render names in familiar English** (Cain, Noah, Moses, Mary), with the
  original form noted in metadata, not the prose.
- **Keep the strangeness** that's actually in the text. Don't sand off the
  ancient, the uncomfortable, or the miraculous to make it palatable; tell it
  straight and let the metadata do the explaining.
- **Translate the world quietly** — if a custom is opaque, convey its weight in a
  phrase rather than stopping to lecture (save the full explanation for
  "Background a modern reader needs").
- **Stay faithful to the events.** You may dramatize gaps (a glance, the weather,
  a silence) to make a scene live, but never invent events that contradict or
  materially add to the text. Flag any notable dramatization in the apparatus.

## Don't

- No "thee/thou," "verily," or mock-King-James cadence.
- No sermonizing, no moral spelled out at the end, no "and so we learn that…".
- No anachronisms in the prose (no clocks, no modern idioms that break the world).
- No smirking, no debunking, no air-quotes around the supernatural.
- No filler ("It is interesting to note that…").

## Copyright (non-negotiable)

- The retelling is **your own original prose, every time.** That is what keeps the
  project clean. Do not lightly reword a copyrighted translation — write it fresh.
- When you must **quote** scripture verbatim (mostly in metadata), use only
  **public-domain** translations — **World English Bible (WEB)** as the readable
  default, **KJV**, or **ASV (1901)** — and keep quotes short.
- **Never** reproduce NIV, ESV, NLT, NASB, CSB, NRSV, *The Message*, or other
  in-copyright translations. Paraphrase their sense in your own words instead.

## A note on the hardest cases

- **Poetry (Psalms, etc.):** don't narrate it. Render it as modern poetry or
  cadenced prose that keeps the imagery and movement, then let metadata carry the
  rest.
- **Law:** render plainly what the rule required and the world it assumed; the
  "story" is the human situation behind the rule.
- **Letters (epistles):** paraphrase the argument in plain modern English as if
  the writer were explaining it to a friend today — faithful to the logic, not a
  verse-by-verse copy.
