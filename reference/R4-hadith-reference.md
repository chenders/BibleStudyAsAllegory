# R4 — The Hadith: Collections Reference

The *hadith* — the recorded sayings, actions, and tacit approvals of the Prophet
Muhammad — and the major collections that preserve them. After the Quran (`R3`),
hadith are the second foundation of Islamic law (*fiqh*), theology, and practice
(*sunna*, "the trodden path"). This file explains what hadith are, the science by
which they are graded, the principal Sunni and Shia collections, how they are
organized, and the most famous individual reports — with cross-links to biblical
material (`R1`) where narratives overlap.

**Companion files:** `R0` rubric · `R1` Bible · `R2` Torah · `R3` Quran ·
`R5` ANE sources.

## What a hadith is

Every hadith has two parts:
- the **isnad** (*sanad*) — the **chain of transmission**: "A heard from B, who heard from C, who heard the Prophet say…"; and
- the **matn** — the **text/content** of the report itself.
  The isnad is the heart of hadith authentication: a report's reliability is judged
  chiefly by the **unbrokenness of its chain** and the **integrity and memory of each
  narrator**, assessed through *ilm al-rijal* ("the science of men," biographical
  evaluation of transmitters). A special category, **Hadith Qudsi** ("sacred
  hadith"), reports God's words in the Prophet's voice — distinct from the Quran,
  which is God's *recited* and inimitable speech.

**Hadith and the Quran.** Hadith **explain, specify, and extend** the Quran. The
Quran commands prayer but does not detail its mechanics; the hadith supply the
number of cycles, postures, and times. Schools differ on how far hadith may
**add to** the Quran, and on which reports are binding — a central question in
*usul al-fiqh* (legal theory).
 
---

## The grading of hadith (authentication)

Classical Sunni scholarship grades a hadith chiefly by its chain:

- **Sahih** (صحيح, "sound") — an unbroken chain of fully reliable, accurate narrators, free of defect or irregularity. Fully authoritative.
- **Hasan** (حسن, "good") — like sahih but with a slightly lesser degree in a narrator's precision. Acceptable as legal evidence.
- **Da'if** (ضعيف, "weak") — a break in the chain or a problematic narrator. Not used to establish law, though many scholars permit it for moral encouragement (*fada'il al-a'mal*).
- (Further sub-grades exist — *mutawatir*, "mass-transmitted" and certain; *ahad*, "single-chain"; *mawdu'*, "fabricated.")
  **Shia (Imami/Twelver) grading** adds a fourth principal category and a different
  decisive criterion:

- **Sahih** — all narrators reliable **and Imami** (Twelver Shia).
- **Hasan** — reliable Imami narrators, but without explicit attestation of *all*.
- **Muwaththaq** (موثق, "reliable/trustworthy") — the chain includes a narrator who is **reliable but not Imami** (e.g. of another school). *(This category was systematized by Ibn Tawus, d. 1265.)*
- **Da'if** — otherwise weak.
  The defining Shia difference is **sectarian affiliation**: transmission through, or
  endorsed by, the **infallible Imams** (especially Ja'far al-Sadiq, d. 765) is
  central, and a narrator's Imami status weighs heavily. **Usuli** scholars examine
  each report individually (the Four Books are authoritative but not wholly
  infallible); **Akhbari** scholars accept their contents more comprehensively.

## The Sunni collections — the Kutub al-Sittah ("The Six Books")

Compiled in the 9th–10th centuries, when hadith were systematically gathered,
sifted, and graded. The "six" grouping was formalized later (by Ibn al-Qaisarani,
11th c.). Listed with compiler and death date:

> **1. Sahih al-Bukhari** — Muhammad al-Bukhari (d. 870). The most authoritative Sunni collection; contains only material its author judged *sahih*, selected from a vast pool. Its first hadith ("actions are but by intentions") is among the most cited in Islam. Foremost commentary: Ibn Hajar's *Fath al-Bari*.

> **2. Sahih Muslim** — Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 875). The second great *sahih*, prized for the rigor of its chains and arrangement. Commentary: al-Nawawi's *Sharh*. Bukhari and Muslim together are the *Sahihayn*, the gold standard; reports in both are *muttafaq alayh* ("agreed upon").

> **3. Sunan Abu Dawud** — Abu Dawud al-Sijistani (d. 889). A *sunan* (arranged by legal topic), focused on hadith bearing on law.

> **4. Jami' al-Tirmidhi** — al-Tirmidhi (d. 892). Notable for recording the **grade** of each hadith and the legal opinions attached — an early model of critical apparatus.

> **5. Sunan al-Nasa'i** (al-Sunan al-Sughra) — al-Nasa'i (d. 915). Esteemed for the strictness of its narrator criteria.

> **6. Sunan Ibn Majah** — Ibn Majah (d. 887). The last added to the six; contains some weaker material. *(Some authorities substitute Malik's* **Muwatta** *— an early law-and-hadith compendium, d. 795 — for Ibn Majah.)*

*Note:* even the two *Sahihs* were subjected to targeted critique (al-Daraqutni,
d. 995, examined a number of their reports) — authentication is an ongoing science,
not a closed verdict.

## The Shia collections — the Four Books (al-Kutub al-Arba'a)

The core of Twelver Shia hadith, compiled by "the Three Muhammads." The collective
label "Four Books" is a later (post-15th-c.) designation.

> **1. Al-Kafi** — Muhammad al-Kulayni (d. 941). The largest and most important, with roughly **16,000 narrations** (figures vary by edition), in three parts: **Usul al-Kafi** (theology and doctrine), **Furu al-Kafi** (law/practice), and **Rawdat al-Kafi** (miscellany).

> **2. Man la yahduruhu al-Faqih** ("For one without a jurist at hand") — Ibn Babawayh, "al-Shaykh al-Saduq" (d. 991). A practical legal reference.

> **3. Tahdhib al-Ahkam** — al-Shaykh al-Tusi (d. 1067). A large legal commentary-collection.

> **4. Al-Istibsar** — also al-Tusi. A focused work reconciling apparently conflicting reports.

Shia hadith also transmit the words of the **Twelve Imams**, not only the Prophet,
and prize works like **Nahj al-Balagha** (sayings and sermons attributed to Ali).

## How collections are organized — the *kitab* structure

Most collections are arranged by **kitab** (book/topic), each subdivided into *bab*
(chapters). A typical topical sequence:

*Revelation & Faith (iman)* · *Knowledge (ilm)* · *Purification (tahara)* ·
*Prayer (salat)* · *Almsgiving (zakat)* · *Fasting (sawm)* · *Pilgrimage (hajj)* ·
*Transactions/sales (buyu')* · *Marriage (nikah)* · *Divorce (talaq)* ·
*Inheritance (fara'id)* · *Oaths & vows* · *Foods, drinks, dress* · *Jihad* ·
*Judgments (ahkam)* · *Virtues (fada'il)* · *Heart-softeners (riqaq)* ·
*Tribulations & the end times (fitan)*.

A *sunan* is organized to mirror the chapters of legal manuals (so a jurist can find
the relevant reports); a *musnad* (e.g. that of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, d. 855) is instead
organized **by narrator**.

## Famous individual hadith

> **The Hadith of Gabriel** — Gabriel, in human form, questions the Prophet, defining **islam** (the five pillars), **iman** (the six articles of faith), and **ihsan** ("to worship God as if you see Him"). Often called the summary of the whole religion. *(Bukhari; Muslim.)*

> **"Actions are by intentions"** (*innama al-a'mal bi-l-niyyat*) — the opening hadith of Sahih al-Bukhari; a foundational maxim of Islamic ethics and law.

> **The Forty Hadith of al-Nawawi** — a beloved anthology (actually 42) of pithy, foundational reports compiled by al-Nawawi (d. 1277), memorized worldwide.

> **The Farewell Sermon** — the Prophet's final address at Arafat, on the sanctity of life and property, the end of tribal vendetta, and the rights of women.

> **Hadith Qudsi** examples — "My mercy prevails over My wrath"; the report that God draws near to the one who draws near to Him. Divine first-person sayings outside the Quran.

> **"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."** A widely cited ethical hadith — close in spirit to the "golden rule" and to Leviticus 19:18 (`→R1`).

## Parallels to biblical material

Hadith and the broader *sunna* literature carry narratives that overlap with the
Bible (`R1`) and elaborate Quranic prophet-stories (`R3`):

- **The Isra and Mi'raj** — the Prophet's Night Journey to Jerusalem and ascent through the heavens, where he meets earlier prophets (Adam, Abraham, Moses, Jesus). Resonates with apocalyptic ascent literature (cf. 1 Enoch, `→R5`).
- **Qisas al-Anbiya** ("Stories of the Prophets") — a genre, drawing on hadith and tradition, retelling the lives of Adam, Noah, Abraham, Joseph, Moses, David, Solomon, Jonah, Jesus, and others, often filling narrative gaps in the Quran with material paralleling Jewish *aggadah* and *midrash* (`→R2`) and biblical narrative (`→R1`).
- **Eschatological hadith** (the *fitan* literature) — signs of the Hour, the Dajjal (a false-messiah figure), the return of Jesus, Gog and Magog — paralleling biblical apocalyptic (`→R1` Daniel, Revelation; `→R5` for the ANE/Persian background of apocalyptic motifs).
  These are documented as **shared narrative tradition** across the Abrahamic
  corpora, not claims of literary dependence in either direction; the relationships
  are debated and are flagged as such.
