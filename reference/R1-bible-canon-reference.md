# R1 — The Bible: Canon Reference Guide (Protestant 66 Books)

A book-by-book, story-by-story reference to the Protestant canon: what each unit
is, where it sits, what it says, what it connects to (inside the Bible and across
the companion corpora), a keyword taxonomy, and a four-axis score per unit.

**Companion files:** `R0` scoring rubric & taxonomy · `R2` Torah (Jewish framing) ·
`R3` Quran · `R4` Hadith · `R5` Ancient Near Eastern sources. This file is the
Christian-canon catalogue; the same Pentateuch read in its own Jewish framing
(parashot, Tanakh order, midrash) lives in `R2`, and cross-corpus parallels
(Flood ↔ Gilgamesh, etc.) are detailed in `R5`. Tags like `→ANE:Gilgamesh` point there.
 
---

## How to read an entry

Each unit is formatted:

> **Traditional title — descriptive subtitle** · `Book ch:v–v` · *genre*
> What the unit contains and says (a few sentences).
> **Related:** intra-biblical parallels & cross-corpus links. **Keywords:** taxonomy tags. **Score:** `I# · C# · P# · H# · A#`

**Score key** (full definitions in `R0`): **I** Influence/cultural afterlife ·
**C** Contestedness · **P** Popularity/familiarity · **H** Harmful reception ·
**A** Historical attestation. Each 0–5, scored independently. Scores are
evidence-anchored editorial judgments, refreshable at review.

*Migration note:* the fifth axis (**A**) was added after this catalogue's first
pass. Entries below show the original four and gain an **A** score at their next
review refresh; the flagship **Cain and Abel** entry is updated as the model.

**Granularity.** Following the Operation Guide: one coherent episode = one entry.
Genealogies, census lists, repetitive legal sections, and dense oracle collections
are **grouped** rather than atomized, and flagged as such. The most influential and
familiar units get the fullest treatment.

**Neutrality & copyright.** Descriptions are original prose. Interpretations are
attributed to traditions/schools, not asserted as settled. Harmful reception is
recorded as documented misuse, framed and rejected. No in-copyright translation is
reproduced.

**A note on authorship/dating.** Each book header gives both the **traditional**
ascription and the **mainstream critical** view, presented as the two positions
they are. The critical frameworks (the Documentary Hypothesis for the Torah, the
Deuteronomistic History, Two- and Three-Isaiah, Markan priority and Q) are
themselves debated; the headers note this.

# OLD TESTAMENT

The 39 books in Christian order: the **Pentateuch** (Genesis–Deuteronomy), the
**Historical books** (Joshua–Esther), the **Wisdom & Poetry** (Job–Song of Songs),
the **Major Prophets** (Isaiah–Daniel), and the **Minor Prophets** (Hosea–Malachi).
For the Jewish three-part order (Torah / Nevi'im / Ketuvim) and the 24-book count,
see `R2`.

## GENESIS — *"In the beginning"*

**Genre:** primeval myth-of-origins + patriarchal narrative + genealogy.
**Traditional authorship/date:** Moses (15th–13th c. BCE).
**Critical view:** a composite of sources — classically **J** (Yahwist), **E**
(Elohist), and **P** (Priestly) — woven and shaped across the monarchic-to-exilic
period, reaching final form around or after the Babylonian exile (6th–5th c. BCE).
The four-source scheme is mainstream but contested today by supplementary and
fragmentary models. **Structure:** Primeval history (1–11) → Abraham (12–25) →
Isaac & Jacob (26–36) → Joseph (37–50). The hinge is the call of Abram (12): from
universal origins to one family's story.

### Primeval history (Gen 1–11)

> **The Seven Days of Creation — order out of chaos** · `Gen 1:1–2:3` · *cosmogonic narrative / liturgy (P)*
> God speaks the ordered cosmos into being over six days and rests on the seventh, making each domain then filling it, and crowning creation with humankind "in the image of God," male and female, given dominion. The refrain "and God saw that it was good" structures the whole; the seventh-day rest grounds the Sabbath.
> **Related:** the second creation account follows in Gen 2; chaos-waters (*tehom*) echo `→ANE:Enuma-Elish` (Tiamat); creation-by-speech echoes Egyptian Memphite theology. **Keywords:** `creation` `myth-of-origins` `liturgy` `kingship` `→ANE:Enuma-Elish`. **Score:** `I5 · C5 · P5 · H1`
> *(C5: the literal-six-day-vs-figurative split is a defining modern fault line; H1: occasional "dominion" misuse in environmental debate.)*

> **The Garden of Eden — the second creation account** · `Gen 2:4–25` · *narrative (J)*
> A second, earthier telling: God forms the man from dust, plants a garden, makes the animals, and fashions the woman from the man's side as a partner. The order and texture differ from Gen 1 (man first, then plants and animals, then woman), which is the classic two-accounts problem.
> **Related:** the Gen 1 vs Gen 2 doublet is a textbook source-critical case. **Keywords:** `creation` `myth-of-origins` `Eden` `Adam` `Eve`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P4 · H2`
> *(H2: Gen 2 used in some traditions to argue female subordination — documented, contested.)*

> **The Fall and Expulsion — the first disobedience** · `Gen 3` · *narrative (J)*
> The serpent persuades the woman to eat from the forbidden tree; the man eats too; their eyes open to shame; God pronounces consequences (pain, toil, enmity, mortality) and expels them from Eden, posting cherubim at the gate. The seedbed of the Christian doctrine of original sin.
> **Related:** echoed in Romans 5 (Adam/Christ) and 1 Tim 2; the serpent reread as Satan in later tradition (Rev 12). **Keywords:** `fall` `sin` `disobedience` `Eden` `judgment` `mercy-within-judgment`. **Score:** `I5 · C4 · P5 · H3`
> *(H3: "Eve first deceived" used across centuries to justify misogyny — documented misuse, framed and rejected.)*

> **Cain and Abel — the first murder** · `Gen 4:1–16` · *narrative (J)*
> Two brothers bring offerings; God favors Abel's; Cain, resentful, ignores God's warning that "sin is crouching at the door" and kills his brother in the field, then dodges with "Am I my brother's keeper?" God curses and yet protects him with a mark. *(See the project's full worked example for the complete treatment.)*
> **Related:** alluded to in Heb 11:4, 12:24; 1 John 3:12; Jude 11; `→quran` 5:27–32 (the raven detail); `→ANE:Dumuzid-Enkimdu` (shepherd-vs-farmer type-scene). **Keywords:** `fratricide` `sibling-rivalry` `sin` `unmastered-anger` `mercy-within-judgment` `blood-cries-out` `Cain` `Abel`. **Score:** `I5 · C4 · P5 · H2 · A0`
> *(A0: primeval/etiological — no testable historical claim; see the worked example's* Is it historical? *field.)*
> *(H2: racialized "mark of Cain" and the Augustine "Cain = the Jews" trope — both documented misuses, framed and rejected.)*

> **Cain's line and Lamech — the first city and the first boast** · `Gen 4:17–26` · *genealogy + narrative*
> Cain's descendants found a city and the arts (herding, music, metalwork); Lamech sings a chilling boast of escalated vengeance. Then Seth is born, and people "began to call on the name of the LORD."
> **Related:** the two-Lamechs / two-Enochs name collisions (cf. Gen 5). **Keywords:** `genealogy` `kingship` `violence` `Cain`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

> **From Adam to Noah — the ten long-lived patriarchs** · `Gen 5` · *genealogy*
> A genealogy from Adam to Noah with extraordinary lifespans (Methuselah, 969), and the cryptic note that Enoch "walked with God, and was not, for God took him."
> **Related:** the long pre-flood reigns parallel the `→ANE:Sumerian-King-List`; Enoch's translation seeds 1 Enoch and Heb 11:5. **Keywords:** `genealogy` `→ANE:Sumerian-King-List`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

> **The sons of God and the Nephilim — heaven and earth transgress** · `Gen 6:1–8` · *narrative fragment*
> The "sons of God" take human wives and produce the Nephilim ("giants"); God limits human lifespan and grieves over human wickedness. A famously obscure passage.
> **Related:** expanded massively in 1 Enoch (the Watchers); cf. Jude 6, 2 Pet 2:4. **Keywords:** `myth-of-origins` `judgment` `theophany`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P2 · H1`

> **Noah and the Flood — the world unmade and remade** · `Gen 6:9–9:17` · *narrative (J+P composite)*
> God, grieved by violence, resolves to flood the earth but saves righteous Noah, his family, and pairs of animals in an ark. The waters rise and recede; Noah sends out birds; the ark rests on Ararat; Noah sacrifices, and God sets the rainbow as a covenant never again to flood the earth. Internal doublets (pairs vs. sevens of animals; flood durations) are the classic case for the J/P weave.
> **Related:** the strongest ANE parallel of all — `→ANE:Gilgamesh` Tablet XI (Utnapishtim) and `→ANE:Atrahasis`; `→quran` (Nuh; a son drowns). **Keywords:** `flood` `judgment` `covenant` `mercy-within-judgment` `Noah` `Ararat` `→ANE:Gilgamesh` `→ANE:Atrahasis`. **Score:** `I5 · C4 · P5 · H1`

> **Noah's drunkenness and the curse of Canaan** · `Gen 9:18–29` · *narrative*
> Noah plants a vineyard, gets drunk, and lies uncovered; Ham "sees"; Noah curses Ham's son Canaan to servitude and blesses Shem and Japheth. The text curses *Canaan*, not Ham, and says nothing of skin color.
> **Related:** the foundation of the "Curse of Ham." **Keywords:** `curse` `judgment` `Noah`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H5`
> *(H5: the "Curse of Ham," conflated with the mark of Cain, was a central proslavery and segregationist proof-text for the Atlantic slave trade — documented misuse with no basis in the text; framed and rejected. See Goldenberg, *The Curse of Ham*.)*

> **The Table of Nations — the peoples of the world** · `Gen 10` · *genealogy*
> A genealogical map of the known nations descended from Noah's three sons — an ancient ethnography in family-tree form.
> **Related:** undone/contextualized by Babel (Gen 11). **Keywords:** `genealogy` `myth-of-origins`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H1`

> **The Tower of Babel — the scattering of tongues** · `Gen 11:1–9` · *etiological narrative (J)*
> Humanity, speaking one language, builds a city and tower "to make a name"; God confuses their speech and scatters them. An etiology of linguistic and national diversity, and a critique of imperial hubris (Babel = Babylon).
> **Related:** reversed at Pentecost (Acts 2). **Keywords:** `myth-of-origins` `judgment` `kingship` `Babel`. **Score:** `I4 · C2 · P4 · H2 · A0`
> *(A0: primeval/etiological — no datable claim. H2 [arguably H3]: the Dutch Reformed "separate development" reading drew on Babel + the Table of Nations [Gen 10] to defend apartheid — documented misuse, repudiated as heresy [Belhar, 1982]; recorded as rejected reception. Final H and the full Text→Reception→Consequence→Counter-tradition→Status block belong in the deep entry, owned by #11.)*

> **From Shem to Abram — bridge to the patriarchs** · `Gen 11:10–32` · *genealogy*
> The line from Shem to Terah, with Abram, Sarai, and Lot introduced; the family migrates from Ur toward Canaan and settles in Haran.
> **Related:** sets up Gen 12. **Keywords:** `genealogy` `Ur` `Haran` `Abraham`. **Score:** `I1 · C2 · P1 · H0`

### Abraham cycle (Gen 12–25)

> **The call of Abram — leave, and be a blessing** · `Gen 12:1–9` · *narrative (J)*
> God calls Abram to leave his land for a land he will be shown, promising nationhood, blessing, and that "all families of the earth" will be blessed through him. Abram goes. The foundational covenant promise.
> **Related:** cited as the model of faith in Rom 4, Gal 3, Heb 11; `→quran` (Ibrahim). **Keywords:** `call` `covenant` `promise` `faith` `Abraham` `Canaan`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H1`

> **Abram and Sarai in Egypt — the wife-sister ruse** · `Gen 12:10–20` · *narrative*
> Fleeing famine to Egypt, Abram passes Sarai off as his sister; Pharaoh takes her; God afflicts Pharaoh, who expels them. One of three "wife-sister" doublets (cf. 20; 26).
> **Related:** parallels Gen 20 and 26 — a key source-critical triplet. **Keywords:** `trickster` `Egypt` `Abraham` `Sarah`. **Score:** `I1 · C3 · P1 · H0`

> **Abram and Lot separate; the rescue of Lot; Melchizedek** · `Gen 13–14` · *narrative*
> Abram and Lot part ways over grazing land; Lot settles near Sodom. When kings capture Lot, Abram mounts a rescue and is blessed by Melchizedek, priest-king of Salem, to whom he gives a tithe.
> **Related:** Melchizedek becomes a major Christological type (Ps 110:4; Heb 7). **Keywords:** `covenant` `kingship` `Abraham` `Sodom`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **The covenant of the pieces — a promise sealed in fire** · `Gen 15` · *covenant narrative*
> God promises Abram descendants as numerous as the stars and the land; Abram "believed, and it was reckoned as righteousness." A smoking firepot passes between split animals, sealing the covenant, with a foretold 400-year sojourn and oppression.
> **Related:** Gen 15:6 anchors Paul's doctrine of justification by faith (Rom 4; Gal 3). **Keywords:** `covenant` `promise` `faith` `theophany` `Abraham`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P2 · H0`

> **Hagar and the birth of Ishmael** · `Gen 16` · *narrative*
> Childless Sarai gives her slave Hagar to Abram; Hagar conceives, tensions flare, she flees, and the angel of the LORD meets her at a well, promising her son Ishmael a great line. The first person in the Bible to name God ("the God who sees me").
> **Related:** Gen 21 (Hagar sent away); Gal 4 (Paul's allegory); `→quran`/`→hadith` (Ishmael, Hagar, and Zamzam in Islamic tradition). **Keywords:** `barren-woman-births` `Hagar` `Ishmael` `Abraham`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H1`

> **The covenant of circumcision; new names** · `Gen 17` · *covenant narrative (P)*
> God renames Abram → Abraham and Sarai → Sarah, promises a son through Sarah, and institutes circumcision as the covenant sign.
> **Related:** circumcision debated in Acts 15, Galatians. **Keywords:** `covenant` `ritual-instruction` `promise` `Abraham`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **The three visitors; pleading for Sodom** · `Gen 18` · *narrative (J)*
> Three visitors announce that Sarah will bear a son (she laughs); then Abraham bargains God down over how many righteous would spare Sodom — from fifty to ten. A landmark text on divine justice and intercession.
> **Related:** sets up Gen 19; the laughter puns on "Isaac" (*yitzhaq*). **Keywords:** `theophany` `promise` `judgment` `Abraham` `Sodom`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **Sodom and Gomorrah; Lot's daughters** · `Gen 19` · *narrative*
> Two angels visit Lot in Sodom; the townsmen demand to abuse them; Lot offers his daughters; God destroys the cities with fire; Lot's wife looks back and becomes salt; Lot's daughters, thinking the world ended, conceive by him (origin of Moab and Ammon).
> **Related:** invoked across the prophets and NT (Luke 17; Jude 7); a contested locus in debates over the Bible and homosexuality (the "sin of Sodom" — inhospitality/violence vs. same-sex acts). **Keywords:** `judgment` `Sodom` `Lot`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P4 · H4`
> *(H4: read as a blanket condemnation of homosexuality and used to justify anti-LGBTQ law and violence — a contested reading recorded as documented harmful application; framed and rejected.)*

> **Abraham and Abimelech — the second wife-sister episode** · `Gen 20` · *narrative (E)*
> A near-repeat of Gen 12 at Gerar; God protects Sarah in a dream-warning to Abimelech.
> **Related:** the wife-sister triplet (12; 26). **Keywords:** `trickster` `dream-revelation` `Abraham` `Sarah`. **Score:** `I1 · C3 · P1 · H0`

> **The birth of Isaac; Hagar and Ishmael sent away** · `Gen 21` · *narrative*
> Isaac is born and Sarah laughs with joy; tension with Ishmael leads Sarah to demand Hagar's expulsion; God preserves Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness and renews the promise over Ishmael.
> **Related:** Gen 16; Gal 4:21–31. **Keywords:** `barren-woman-births` `promise` `Isaac` `Ishmael` `Hagar`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P2 · H1`

> **The Binding of Isaac (the Aqedah) — the test** · `Gen 22:1–19` · *narrative (E)*
> God commands Abraham to sacrifice Isaac; Abraham obeys to the brink, knife raised, when the angel stops him and provides a ram. One of the most ethically searing and most-interpreted texts in scripture.
> **Related:** central to Jewish (the Aqedah), Christian (a type of the crucifixion), and Islamic tradition (`→quran` 37, where the son is generally Ishmael); Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling*; reworks the ANE child-sacrifice backdrop (`→R5`). **Keywords:** `testing` `sacrifice` `faith` `obedience` `firstborn` `Abraham` `Isaac` `Moriah` `→ANE:child-sacrifice`. **Score:** `I5 · C4 · P4 · H2 · A2`
> *(H2: invoked to valorize unquestioning obedience in harmful ways — contested. A2: legendary/unverifiable — the patriarchs as individuals lie beyond the reach of evidence; not A0, since it concerns named figures rather than purely primeval/etiological material.)*

> **The death of Sarah; the cave of Machpelah** · `Gen 23` · *narrative*
> Abraham buys a burial cave for Sarah at Hebron — the family's first toehold of owned land in Canaan.
> **Related:** the patriarchal burial site. **Keywords:** `covenant` `Canaan` `Abraham` `Sarah`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H1`

> **A wife for Isaac — Rebekah at the well** · `Gen 24` · *narrative*
> Abraham's servant, guided by prayer and a sign, finds Rebekah at a well; she consents to marry Isaac. The Bible's longest chapter, a courtship set-piece.
> **Related:** the "betrothal at a well" type-scene (cf. Jacob/Rachel, Moses/Zipporah). **Keywords:** `dream-revelation` `providence` `Isaac` `Rebekah`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H0`

> **Abraham's death; the birth of Jacob and Esau; the birthright** · `Gen 25` · *narrative + genealogy*
> Abraham dies; Ishmael's line is noted; Rebekah bears struggling twins, Esau and Jacob, with an oracle that "the elder shall serve the younger"; Esau sells his birthright to Jacob for stew.
> **Related:** Mal 1; Rom 9 ("Jacob I loved, Esau I hated"). **Keywords:** `sibling-rivalry` `younger-favored` `trickster` `Jacob` `Esau`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

### Isaac & Jacob cycle (Gen 26–36)

> **Isaac and Abimelech — the third wife-sister episode** · `Gen 26` · *narrative*
> Isaac repeats his father's ruse at Gerar, prospers, and quarrels over wells; God renews the covenant to him.
> **Related:** completes the wife-sister triplet (12; 20). **Keywords:** `covenant` `trickster` `Isaac`. **Score:** `I1 · C3 · P1 · H0`

> **Jacob's ladder at Bethel** · `Gen 28` · *dream-revelation*

> **Jacob, Leah, Rachel, and the children** · `Gen 29–30` · *narrative*

> **Jacob flees Laban; the covenant at Mizpah** · `Gen 31` · *narrative*

> **Wrestling with God at Peniel — Jacob becomes Israel** · `Gen 32` · *theophany narrative*
> The night before facing Esau, Jacob wrestles a mysterious man until dawn, is wounded in the hip, and is renamed Israel ("strives with God"). He names the place Peniel ("face of God").
> **Related:** Hos 12; the name "Israel" itself. **Keywords:** `theophany` `testing` `Jacob` `Peniel`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **Reconciliation with Esau** · `Gen 33` · *narrative*
> Esau, against expectation, runs to embrace Jacob; the brothers reconcile and part in peace.
> **Keywords:** `reconciliation` `sibling-rivalry` `Jacob` `Esau`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H0`

> **Dinah and Shechem — rape and revenge** · `Gen 34` · *narrative*
> Shechem violates Dinah; her brothers Simeon and Levi, feigning a marriage alliance via circumcision, massacre the city. A dark, unresolved episode.
> **Keywords:** `violence` `trickster` `judgment`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P1 · H1`

> **Return to Bethel; Rachel's death** · `Gen 35` · *narrative*

> **The descendants of Esau (Edom)** · `Gen 36` · *genealogy*

### Joseph cycle (Gen 37–50)

> **Joseph's dreams; sold into slavery** · `Gen 37` · *narrative*
> Jacob's favorite son, Joseph, flaunts his dreams of dominance and his special coat; his jealous brothers throw him in a pit and sell him to traders bound for Egypt, faking his death.
> **Related:** the long, unified Joseph novella; `→quran` Sura 12 (Yusuf), told in full. **Keywords:** `sibling-rivalry` `younger-favored` `dream-revelation` `Joseph` `Egypt` `→quran`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **Judah and Tamar** · `Gen 38` · *narrative*
> An interruption: Tamar, denied her levirate due, disguises herself as a prostitute, conceives by her father-in-law Judah, and is vindicated — "she is more righteous than I."
> **Related:** Tamar in Jesus' genealogy (Matt 1). **Keywords:** `trickster` `levirate` `judgment` `Judah`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **Joseph and Potiphar's wife** · `Gen 39` · *narrative*
> In Egypt Joseph rises in Potiphar's house, refuses the advances of Potiphar's wife, is falsely accused, and is imprisoned — yet "the LORD was with him."
> **Related:** parallels the Egyptian `→ANE:Tale-of-Two-Brothers` (spurned-seductress / false accusation). **Keywords:** `testing` `faith` `Joseph` `Egypt` `→ANE:Tale-of-Two-Brothers`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **The cupbearer and the baker; Pharaoh's dreams; Joseph's rise** · `Gen 40–41` · *narrative*
> Joseph interprets dreams in prison, then interprets Pharaoh's dreams of famine, and is made vizier over Egypt to manage the coming famine.
> **Keywords:** `dream-revelation` `providence` `wisdom-vs-folly` `Joseph` `Egypt`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P3 · H0`

> **The brothers come to Egypt; Joseph reveals himself** · `Gen 42–45` · *narrative*
> Driven by famine, Joseph's brothers come to buy grain, not recognizing him; he tests them with the planted cup and Benjamin's peril, then breaks down and reveals himself — "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good."
> **Keywords:** `reconciliation` `providence` `sibling-rivalry` `Joseph`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P3 · H0`

> **Jacob's family settles in Egypt; Jacob blesses his sons; the deaths of Jacob and Joseph** · `Gen 46–50` · *narrative + poetic blessing*
> Israel's family moves to Egypt under Joseph's protection; the dying Jacob blesses his twelve sons in a tribal oracle-poem (Gen 49); both Jacob and Joseph die, with Joseph foreseeing the exodus ("God will surely visit you").
> **Related:** Gen 49 = the "Blessing of Jacob," a key tribal poem (Judah's scepter, Gen 49:10, read messianically). **Keywords:** `genealogy` `poetry` `providence` `messianic-hope` `Joseph` `Egypt`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

## EXODUS — *"These are the names"*

**Genre:** narrative + law + ritual instruction. **Traditional:** Moses.
**Critical:** J/E/P composite; the law collections (esp. the Covenant Code) and the
tabernacle texts (P) are distinct strata. **Historicity** of the exodus event is
debated — from maximalist to minimalist positions. **Structure:** oppression and
deliverance (1–15) → wilderness journey to Sinai (15–18) → covenant and law at
Sinai (19–24) → tabernacle, golden calf, and presence (25–40).

> **Israel enslaved; the birth of Moses** · `Exod 1–2` · *narrative*
> A new Pharaoh enslaves the multiplying Israelites and orders Hebrew boys killed; Moses is hidden, set adrift, drawn from the Nile by Pharaoh's daughter, raised in the palace, then flees to Midian after killing an Egyptian overseer.
> **Related:** the exposed-infant motif echoes the `→ANE:Sargon` birth legend. **Keywords:** `exodus-deliverance` `Moses` `Egypt` `→ANE:Sargon-legend`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **The burning bush — the call of Moses and the divine name** · `Exod 3:1–4:17` · *theophany narrative*
> At a bush that burns without being consumed, God calls Moses to free Israel and reveals the name **YHWH** ("I AM WHO I AM"); Moses objects, and is given signs and Aaron as spokesman.
> **Related:** the name central to Jewish and Christian theology; John's "I am" sayings. **Keywords:** `call` `theophany` `exodus-deliverance` `Moses` `Sinai/Horeb` `YHWH/God`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **The ten plagues** · `Exod 5–11` · *narrative*
> Pharaoh refuses; God sends ten escalating plagues (blood, frogs, gnats, flies, livestock, boils, hail, locusts, darkness, death of the firstborn), a contest against Egypt's gods, with Pharaoh's heart repeatedly hardened.

## DEUTERONOMY — *"These are the words"*

**Genre:** covenant renewal as farewell speeches + law. **Traditional:** Moses.
**Critical:** the **Deuteronomic** source (D); widely linked to the law-scroll
found under Josiah (2 Kings 22, c. 622 BCE); the literary head of the
"Deuteronomistic History" (Joshua–Kings). **Structure:** three addresses by Moses
on the plains of Moab — historical review (1–11), the Deuteronomic law code
(12–26), blessings/curses and covenant (27–30), and Moses' death (31–34).

> **Moses' first address — remember the wilderness** · `Deut 1–4` · *covenant speech*
> Moses reviews the journey and urges obedience, with the great monotheistic affirmation that the LORD alone is God.
> **Keywords:** `covenant` `wilderness` `Moses`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

> **The Shema and the great commandment** · `Deut 6:4–9` (within 5–11) · *covenant speech*
> "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" — the **Shema**, Judaism's central creed — followed by the command to love God wholly and teach the children. The Decalogue is restated in Deut 5.
> **Related:** quoted by Jesus as the greatest commandment (Mark 12). **Keywords:** `covenant` `love` `liturgy` `law` `Moses`. **Score:** `I5 · C2 · P3 · H0`

> **The Deuteronomic Code** · `Deut 12–26` · *law* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> The central law collection: one sanctuary, festivals, kingship, prophets, courts, war, and social welfare. Includes the *herem* (devotion-to-destruction) war laws (Deut 7; 20).
> **Related:** parallels the Covenant Code (Exodus) and `→ANE:Hammurabi`. **Keywords:** `law` `covenant` `kingship` `war`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P1 · H4`
> *(H4: the herem/conquest laws invoked to justify colonial conquest, genocide, and holy war — documented harmful reception; framed and rejected. See conquest material in Joshua.)*

> **Blessings and curses; choose life** · `Deut 27–30` · *covenant speech*
> The covenant set before Israel as blessing for obedience, curse for disobedience: "I have set before you life and death… therefore choose life." A theological key to the whole Deuteronomistic History.
> **Keywords:** `covenant` `judgment` `blessing-and-curse`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H1`

> **The Song of Moses; the death of Moses** · `Deut 31–34` · *poetry + narrative*
> Moses commissions Joshua, sings a great poem (Deut 32), blesses the tribes, views the promised land from Mount Nebo, and dies — "no prophet has since arisen like Moses."
> **Keywords:** `poetry` `covenant` `Moses`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

# HISTORICAL BOOKS (Joshua–Esther)

The story of Israel in the land: conquest, the judges, the rise and fall of the
monarchy, exile, and return. Joshua–Kings form the **Deuteronomistic History**
(critical view), reading the monarchy through Deuteronomy's covenant lens.
Chronicles–Ezra–Nehemiah are a later, priestly retelling.

## JOSHUA — *conquest and settlement*
**Genre:** narrative. **Traditional:** Joshua. **Critical:** Deuteronomistic, exilic shaping; the conquest's historicity is heavily debated (archaeology suggests gradual emergence more than blitzkrieg).

> **Rahab and the spies; crossing the Jordan** · `Josh 1–5` · *narrative*
> Spies sent to Jericho are sheltered by the prostitute Rahab; Israel crosses the Jordan on dry ground; the men are circumcised and keep Passover. Rahab's scarlet cord is spared.
> **Related:** Rahab in Jesus' genealogy (Matt 1) and faith-list (Heb 11). **Keywords:** `narrative` `faith` `exodus-deliverance` `Jericho` `Rahab`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H1`

> **The fall of Jericho** · `Josh 6` · *narrative*
> Israel marches around Jericho seven days; on the seventh the walls collapse at the shout and trumpet; the city is put to the *herem* (devoted to destruction), Rahab's household spared.
> **Related:** "Joshua fit the battle of Jericho" (spiritual); the herem problem. **Keywords:** `miracle` `war` `judgment` `Jericho`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H3`
> *(H3: the conquest as a template for colonial dispossession.)*

> **Achan's sin; Ai; the covenant; the Gibeonite ruse** · `Josh 7–9` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Achan's theft brings defeat at Ai until purged; the law is renewed; the Gibeonites trick Israel into a treaty.
> **Keywords:** `judgment` `covenant` `trickster` `war`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H1`

> **The sun stands still; the conquest campaigns** · `Josh 10–12` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Joshua's southern and northern campaigns; the sun "stands still" at Gibeon — a famous crux of literal-vs-figurative reading.
> **Keywords:** `war` `miracle` `judgment`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P3 · H3`

> **Dividing the land; Joshua's farewell** · `Josh 13–24` · *narrative + tribal allotment* — **grouped**
> The land apportioned by tribe; cities of refuge and Levitical towns set; Joshua's covenant-renewal at Shechem — "as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD."
> **Keywords:** `covenant` `tribal-allotment` `Joshua`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P2 · H1`

## JUDGES — *the cycle of apostasy and rescue*
**Genre:** narrative. **Critical:** Deuteronomistic; a recurring cycle (sin → oppression → cry → judge → peace → relapse). The book darkens toward chaos: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes."

> **The cycle introduced; Othniel, Ehud, Deborah** · `Judg 1–5` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Early judges, including left-handed Ehud's assassination of Eglon and the prophetess-judge Deborah, whose victory with Barak and Jael is celebrated in the ancient "Song of Deborah" (Judg 5).
> **Related:** Judg 5 is among the oldest Hebrew poems. **Keywords:** `narrative` `war` `judgment` `Deborah`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **Gideon** · `Judg 6–8` · *narrative*
> The reluctant Gideon, tested by the fleece, routs the Midianites with 300 men and torches, then declines kingship but makes an idolatrous ephod.
> **Keywords:** `call` `war` `testing` `Gideon`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P3 · H0`

> **Abimelech; Jephthah's vow** · `Judg 9–12` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Abimelech's bloody bid for kingship; Jephthah's rash vow that costs his daughter's life — a searing text.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `sacrifice` `judgment` `Jephthah`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P2 · H1`

> **Samson and Delilah** · `Judg 13–16` · *narrative*
> The Nazirite strongman Samson — riddles, a jawbone, foxes with torches — is undone by Delilah, who learns his secret (his hair), is blinded and enslaved, and brings the Philistine temple down on himself and his captors.
> **Related:** Milton's *Samson Agonistes*; Saint-Saëns' opera; endless film/art. **Keywords:** `narrative` `strength` `betrayal` `Nazirite` `Samson`. **Score:** `I4 · C2 · P4 · H0`

> **Micah's idol; the outrage at Gibeah** · `Judg 17–21` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> The book's grim coda: idolatry, the Levite's concubine, her rape and dismemberment, and civil war against Benjamin — Israel at its most lawless.
> **Keywords:** `violence` `disobedience` `judgment`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P1 · H2`

## RUTH — *loyalty and redemption*
**Genre:** novella. **Critical:** date debated (monarchic to post-exilic); possibly a counter-voice to post-exilic exclusionism.

> **Ruth — the Moabite who joined Israel** · `Ruth 1–4` · *novella*
> Widowed Ruth clings to her mother-in-law Naomi ("where you go, I will go"), gleans in Boaz's field, claims the levirate redemption, and becomes great-grandmother of David. A quiet story of *hesed* (loyal love) and the inclusion of a foreigner.
> **Related:** Ruth in Jesus' genealogy (Matt 1); read at Shavuot in `→torah`/Jewish practice. **Keywords:** `novella` `loyalty` `redemption` `younger-favored` `Ruth` `David`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P3 · H0`

## 1–2 SAMUEL — *the birth of the monarchy*
**Genre:** narrative. **Critical:** Deuteronomistic, incorporating older sources (the "Court History"/Succession Narrative, 2 Sam 9–20, prized as early historiography).

> **Hannah and the birth of Samuel** · `1 Sam 1–3` · *narrative*
> Barren Hannah prays for a son, dedicates Samuel to the sanctuary, and sings a song of reversal; the boy Samuel hears God call in the night.

> **The ark captured and returned** · `1 Sam 4–7` · *narrative*
> The Philistines capture the ark; it plagues them (the god Dagon topples) until they send it back. "Ichabod" — the glory departed.
> **Keywords:** `judgment` `ark` `Philistines`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H0`

> **Israel demands a king; Saul anointed** · `1 Sam 8–12` · *narrative*
> Against Samuel's warning about kingship's costs, Israel demands a king "like the nations"; Saul is chosen and anointed.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `anointing` `Saul` `Samuel`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **Saul's rejection** · `1 Sam 13–15` · *narrative*
> Saul's unlawful sacrifice and his sparing of Amalek's king bring rejection: "to obey is better than sacrifice." The kingdom will pass to another.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `disobedience` `judgment` `Saul`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H1`

> **David anointed; David and Goliath** · `1 Sam 16–17` · *narrative*
> Samuel secretly anoints the shepherd boy David; David soothes Saul with the harp; then, refusing armor, he fells the Philistine giant Goliath with a sling and a stone. The archetypal underdog victory.
> **Related:** "David and Goliath" = the master idiom for the underdog; Michelangelo, Caravaggio, Donatello. **Keywords:** `anointing` `underdog` `war` `faith` `David` `Goliath`. **Score:** `I5 · C3 · P5 · H0`

> **David and Saul; David and Jonathan; David the fugitive** · `1 Sam 18–31` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Saul's jealousy curdles to murderous pursuit; David's deep friendship-covenant with Jonathan; David spares Saul twice; Saul consults the witch of Endor; Saul and Jonathan die on Gilboa.
> **Related:** the David–Jonathan bond is read variously (covenant friendship; some read homoeroticism). **Keywords:** `kingship` `friendship` `pursuit` `David` `Saul` `Jonathan`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **David's reign; the Davidic covenant** · `2 Sam 1–10` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> David laments Saul and Jonathan, unites the tribes, takes Jerusalem, brings up the ark (dancing before it), and receives God's promise of an everlasting dynasty (2 Sam 7) — the seedbed of messianic hope.
> **Related:** 2 Sam 7 = the Davidic covenant, foundational for messianism; the **Tel Dan Stele** ("House of David," `→ANE:Tel-Dan`). **Keywords:** `kingship` `covenant` `messianic-hope` `Jerusalem` `David` `→ANE:Tel-Dan`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **David and Bathsheba; Nathan's rebuke** · `2 Sam 11–12` · *narrative*
> David takes Bathsheba and engineers her husband Uriah's death; the prophet Nathan traps him with a parable — "you are the man" — and David repents under judgment.
> **Related:** Ps 51 (penitential psalm); a paradigm of prophetic truth-telling to power. **Keywords:** `kingship` `sin` `repentance` `judgment` `David` `Bathsheba` `Nathan`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **Absalom's revolt; David's decline** · `2 Sam 13–24` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> The Succession Narrative's tragedy: Amnon's rape of Tamar, Absalom's revenge and rebellion, his death ("O Absalom, my son"), and David's troubled last acts.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `violence` `rebellion` `David` `Absalom`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H1`

## 1–2 KINGS — *the divided monarchy to exile*
**Genre:** narrative. **Critical:** Deuteronomistic History, judging each king by covenant fidelity, ending in exile.

> **Solomon's wisdom and temple; the Queen of Sheba** · `1 Kgs 1–11` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Solomon asks for wisdom, renders the famous baby-splitting judgment, builds the Temple, hosts the Queen of Sheba, and in old age turns to idolatry — seeding the kingdom's split.
> **Related:** the "judgment of Solomon" is a major art subject; Sheba in `→quran` (Bilqis). **Keywords:** `kingship` `wisdom-vs-folly` `temple` `Solomon` `Jerusalem`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **The kingdom divides; Jeroboam's calves** · `1 Kgs 12–16` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> After Solomon, the north (Israel) breaks from the south (Judah); Jeroboam sets up golden calves; a parade of kings "did evil."
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `idolatry` `judgment` `schism`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H0`

> **Elijah — drought, Carmel, the still small voice** · `1 Kgs 17–19` · *narrative*
> The prophet Elijah declares a drought, is fed by ravens and the widow of Zarephath (whose son he revives), defeats the prophets of Baal in a fire contest on Mount Carmel, then meets God not in wind or quake but in "a still small voice."
> **Related:** Baal context → `→ANE:Baal-Cycle`; Elijah expected to return (Mal 4; the Seder cup; John the Baptist). **Keywords:** `prophecy` `miracle` `theophany` `idolatry` `Elijah` `→ANE:Baal-Cycle`. **Score:** `I4 · C2 · P4 · H0`

> **Naboth's vineyard; Ahab and Jezebel** · `1 Kgs 20–22` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Jezebel engineers the judicial murder of Naboth for his vineyard; Elijah pronounces doom on Ahab's house. A classic text on royal abuse of power.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `injustice` `judgment` `Jezebel` `Ahab`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P2 · H0`

> **Elijah taken up; Elisha's miracles** · `2 Kgs 1–8` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Elijah ascends in a fiery chariot, his mantle falling to Elisha, who works healings and provisions, including curing Naaman the Syrian's leprosy.
> **Keywords:** `prophecy` `miracle` `Elijah` `Elisha`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P3 · H0`

> **Jehu's purge; the fall of the north** · `2 Kgs 9–17` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Jehu's bloody coup wipes out Ahab's house and Baal worship; Assyria destroys the northern kingdom (722 BCE) and deports it — explained as covenant judgment.
> **Related:** the "lost ten tribes." **Keywords:** `kingship` `judgment` `exile` `Assyria`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H1`

> **Hezekiah, Josiah, and the fall of Judah** · `2 Kgs 18–25` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Reformer kings Hezekiah (Assyrian siege lifted) and Josiah (the law-scroll found, sweeping reform) bracket Judah's decline; Babylon destroys Jerusalem and the Temple and deports Judah (586 BCE). The book ends in exile with a flicker of hope.
> **Related:** Josiah's scroll linked to Deuteronomy; the exile is the great hinge of the OT. **Keywords:** `kingship` `judgment` `exile` `temple` `Babylon` `Josiah`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H1`

## 1–2 CHRONICLES — *the priestly retelling*
**Genre:** genealogy + narrative. **Critical:** post-exilic; retells Samuel–Kings with a Davidic/Temple focus, omitting David's and Solomon's failures.

> **The genealogies** · `1 Chr 1–9` · *genealogy* — **grouped**
> An immense genealogy from Adam through the tribes — a post-exilic community reconnecting to its roots.
> **Keywords:** `genealogy`. **Score:** `I1 · C2 · P0 · H0`

> **David and Solomon idealized; the Temple** · `1 Chr 10 – 2 Chr 9` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> David and Solomon recast as model kings devoted to the Temple and worship; the focus is liturgical and dynastic.
> **Related:** the Kings/Chronicles "doublet" is the OT's major intra-biblical parallel axis. **Keywords:** `kingship` `temple` `liturgy` `David` `Solomon`. **Score:** `I2 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **The kings of Judah to the exile and Cyrus's decree** · `2 Chr 10–36` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> The Judahite line through to Babylon, ending — unlike Kings — on the hopeful note of Cyrus's decree to rebuild.
> **Keywords:** `kingship` `judgment` `exile` `remnant`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

## EZRA–NEHEMIAH — *return and rebuilding*

> **Ezra's reforms; the mixed-marriage crisis** · `Ezra 7–10` · *narrative* — **grouped**

> **Nehemiah rebuilds the walls; covenant renewal** · `Neh 1–13` · *memoir* — **grouped**

> **Esther — the queen who saved her people** · `Esth 1–10` · *novella*

# WISDOM & POETRY (Job–Song of Songs)

Israel's reflective and lyrical literature: the problem of suffering, the worship
and lament of the Psalms, practical and skeptical wisdom, and love poetry.

## JOB — *the suffering of the righteous*
**Genre:** wisdom + poetic dialogue + theophany. **Critical:** date debated; a prose
frame (1–2; 42) around a poetic core. The Bible's most sustained wrestling with theodicy.

> **The prologue — the wager** · `Job 1–2` · *narrative frame*
> In the heavenly court, "the satan" (the accuser) challenges God that the blameless Job is only pious because he's blessed; God permits Job's children, wealth, and health to be stripped away. Job mourns but "did not sin."
> **Related:** the satan here is a heavenly functionary, not yet the devil — cf. Persian/`→ANE:Zoroastrian` influence on the later figure. **Keywords:** `theodicy` `testing` `suffering-righteous` `Job` `satan`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P3 · H0`

> **The dialogues — Job and his friends** · `Job 3–37` · *poetic dialogue* — **grouped**
> Job laments his birth; three friends (then Elihu) insist suffering must be deserved; Job protests his innocence and demands an audience with God. A profound debate on whether the world is morally fair.
> **Related:** parallels `→ANE:Ludlul` ("the Babylonian Job") and the Babylonian Theodicy — a shared genre of the righteous sufferer. **Keywords:** `theodicy` `wisdom` `lament` `suffering-righteous` `→ANE:Ludlul`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P2 · H0`

> **God answers from the whirlwind** · `Job 38–42` · *theophany + poetry*
> God responds not with explanation but with overwhelming questions about creation — Leviathan, Behemoth, the foundations of the earth. Job is silenced and restored. The answer is awe, not a reason.
> **Related:** Leviathan echoes Canaanite Litan (`→ANE:Baal-Cycle`); a touchstone for theology of mystery. **Keywords:** `theophany` `theodicy` `creation` `wisdom` `Job` `→ANE:Baal-Cycle`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P3 · H0`

## PSALMS — *the hymnbook of Israel*
**Genre:** poetry (hymn, lament, thanksgiving, royal, wisdom, imprecatory). **Structure:**
150 psalms in five "books." Treated as a collection with the most influential psalms
broken out individually below; the rest are grouped by type.

> **The Psalter as a whole** · `Ps 1–150` · *poetry collection* — **grouped overview**
> 150 poems of praise, complaint, trust, and instruction — Israel's prayers, and the most-used liturgical text in Judaism and Christianity. Genres: laments (the largest group), hymns of praise, thanksgivings, royal psalms, wisdom psalms, and the difficult imprecatory (curse) psalms.
> **Keywords:** `psalm` `poetry` `liturgy` `lament` `hymn`. **Score:** `I5 · C2 · P4 · H1`

> **Psalm 1 — the two ways** · `Ps 1` · *wisdom psalm*
> The righteous, like a tree by water, vs. the wicked, like chaff — the gateway to the Psalter.

> **Psalm 22 — "My God, why have you forsaken me?"** · `Ps 22` · *lament*
> A cry of dereliction that turns to praise; "they pierced my hands and feet… they divide my garments."

> **Psalm 23 — "The LORD is my shepherd"** · `Ps 23` · *psalm of trust*

> **Psalm 110 — "The LORD said to my Lord"** · `Ps 110` · *royal psalm*

**Genre:** skeptical wisdom. **Critical:** late (Persian/Hellenistic) Hebrew; "Qoheleth" (the Teacher).

> **The vanity of life under the sun** · `Eccl 1–12` · *wisdom* — **grouped**

> **The Song of Songs** · `Song 1–8` · *love poetry* — **grouped**

> **Related:** read at Passover in `→torah`/Jewish practice; mystical readings (Bernard of Clairvaux, John of the Cross). **Keywords:** `poetry` `love` `allegory`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P2 · H0`

# THE PROPHETS (Isaiah–Malachi)

Prophetic books mix narrated episodes with collected oracles (poetry of warning,
judgment, and hope). Oracles are grouped at a reasonable grain; the most
influential passages are broken out.

## ISAIAH — *judgment and consolation*
**Genre:** prophecy + oracle + narrative. **Critical:** widely divided into First Isaiah
(1–39, 8th c., pre-exilic Jerusalem), Second Isaiah (40–55, exilic consolation), and
Third Isaiah (56–66, post-exilic) — the "Three Isaiahs" hypothesis (contested by
those holding single authorship). The most theologically and culturally loaded prophet.

> **First Isaiah — oracles of judgment; the call; Immanuel** · `Isa 1–39` · *oracle + narrative* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> Judgment on Judah's injustice and idolatry, with flashes of hope: Isaiah's throne-room call ("Holy, holy, holy," ch. 6), the **Immanuel** sign (7:14), the "Prince of Peace" child (9), the peaceable kingdom and "swords into plowshares" (2; 11), and the Hezekiah narratives (36–39).
> **Related:** 7:14 and 9:6 read as messianic by Christians (Matt 1; Handel's *Messiah*); "swords into plowshares" at the UN. **Keywords:** `prophecy` `oracle` `judgment` `messianic-hope` `Isaiah`. **Score:** `I5 · C4 · P4 · H0`

> **Second Isaiah — comfort and the Servant Songs** · `Isa 40–55` · *oracle* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> "Comfort, comfort my people" — exilic consolation, the incomparable creator, and the four **Servant Songs**, climaxing in the **Suffering Servant** (52:13–53:12), who "was wounded for our transgressions."
> **Related:** Isa 53 is the OT's most important Christian Passion text (Acts 8; Handel's *Messiah*); Jewish reading takes the Servant as Israel. A defining interpretive battleground. **Keywords:** `oracle` `consolation` `suffering-righteous` `messianic-hope` `Isaiah`. **Score:** `I5 · C5 · P3 · H1`

> **Third Isaiah — the new heavens and new earth** · `Isa 56–66` · *oracle* — **grouped**
> Post-exilic oracles of inclusion (foreigners and eunuchs welcomed), justice (the fast God chooses, ch. 58), and cosmic renewal ("new heavens and a new earth").
> **Related:** Luke 4 (Jesus reads Isa 61); Rev 21. **Keywords:** `oracle` `restoration` `new-creation` `justice` `Isaiah`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

## JEREMIAH — *the weeping prophet*
**Genre:** prophecy + oracle + narrative + lament. **Critical:** complex; a prose
(Deuteronomistic) layer and poetic oracles; the Greek version is shorter.

> **Jeremiah's call and oracles of doom** · `Jer 1–25` · *oracle + narrative* — **grouped**
> Called from the womb, Jeremiah warns of Babylonian judgment, clashes with false prophets, and suffers for it — the "confessions" voice his anguish.
> **Keywords:** `prophecy` `call` `judgment` `lament` `Jeremiah`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **The new covenant** · `Jer 31:31–34` (within 26–33) · *oracle*
> Amid the "Book of Consolation," the promise of a **new covenant** written on the heart — a pivotal text.
> **Related:** the NT's name ("New Testament") derives from it; Heb 8. **Keywords:** `oracle` `covenant` `restoration` `Jeremiah`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **The fall of Jerusalem and aftermath** · `Jer 34–52` · *narrative + oracle* — **grouped**
> The siege, Jerusalem's fall, Jeremiah's mistreatment, the flight to Egypt, and oracles against the nations.
> **Keywords:** `judgment` `exile` `Babylon` `Jeremiah`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P2 · H0`

## LAMENTATIONS — *the dirge over Jerusalem*
> **Lamentations** · `Lam 1–5` · *acrostic lament* — **grouped**
> Five acrostic poems grieving the destroyed Jerusalem, yet "his mercies are new every morning" (3:22–23).
> **Related:** read on Tisha B'Av (`→torah`/Jewish calendar); the hymn "Great Is Thy Faithfulness." **Keywords:** `lament` `poetry` `exile` `Jerusalem`. **Score:** `I3 · C2 · P2 · H0`

## EZEKIEL — *visions in exile*
**Genre:** prophecy + apocalyptic vision. **Critical:** exilic, Babylon; vivid, bizarre imagery.

> **The chariot vision and the call** · `Ezek 1–3` · *vision*
> The wheels-within-wheels throne-chariot (*merkavah*) and Ezekiel's commissioning to eat the scroll.
> **Related:** foundational to Jewish *merkavah* mysticism. **Keywords:** `vision` `theophany` `call` `Ezekiel`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **Sign-acts and oracles of judgment** · `Ezek 4–24` · *oracle + sign-act* — **grouped**
> Dramatic enacted prophecies and oracles on Jerusalem's coming fall and the departure of God's glory.
> **Keywords:** `prophecy` `judgment` `sign-act` `Ezekiel`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

> **The valley of dry bones; the new temple** · `Ezek 33–48` · *vision + oracle* — **grouped, with flagged unit**
> Restoration oracles, the **valley of dry bones** coming to life (37 — resurrection of the nation), the cosmic Gog-Magog battle (38–39), and the visionary new temple.
> **Related:** "Dem Bones" spiritual; Gog-Magog in apocalyptic/end-times rhetoric; Rev 20. **Keywords:** `vision` `restoration` `resurrection` `apocalyptic` `Ezekiel`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H1`

## DANIEL — *court tales and apocalypse*
**Genre:** narrative (1–6) + apocalyptic (7–12). **Critical:** the apocalypse widely
dated to the Maccabean crisis (c. 165 BCE); a key text for Persian/Hellenistic-era
ideas of resurrection and angels.

> **The court tales — the fiery furnace, the lions' den, the writing on the wall** · `Dan 1–6` · *narrative* — **grouped**
> Daniel and friends keep faith in Babylon: the three thrown into the furnace, Nebuchadnezzar's dream and madness, Belshazzar's feast and "the writing on the wall," and Daniel in the lions' den.
> **Related:** idioms "feet of clay," "writing on the wall"; endless art. **Keywords:** `narrative` `faith` `testing` `dream-revelation` `Daniel` `Babylon`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **The visions — the Son of Man and the end** · `Dan 7–12` · *apocalyptic* — **grouped**
> Symbolic beasts, the "one like a son of man," the seventy weeks, and the promise of resurrection ("many who sleep… shall awake") — the OT's clearest resurrection text and a fountainhead of apocalyptic.
> **Related:** "Son of Man" central to the Gospels; influence of `→ANE:Zoroastrian` eschatology; Revelation. **Keywords:** `apocalyptic` `vision` `resurrection` `end-times` `messianic-hope` `Daniel`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P3 · H1`

# NEW TESTAMENT

The 27 books: the four **Gospels** + **Acts**, the **Pauline letters**, the
**General (Catholic) letters**, and **Revelation**.

## THE GOSPELS — four portraits of Jesus

**The Synoptic Problem.** Matthew, Mark, and Luke ("the Synoptics") share much
material in similar order; John is distinct. The dominant solution is the **Two-Source
Hypothesis**: Mark wrote first (Markan priority), and Matthew and Luke each used Mark
plus a lost sayings source **Q** (the ~200 verses they share that aren't in Mark).
Material unique to Matthew = **M**; unique to Luke = **L**. Rivals: the **Farrer
Hypothesis** (no Q; Luke used Matthew) and the **Griesbach/Two-Gospel** (Matthean
priority). Below, episodes are tagged by which Gospels carry them — **[triple]** =
all three Synoptics, **[Mt/Lk]** = double tradition (Q-type), **[Mt]**, **[Lk]**,
**[Jn]** = unique. Per the methodology, shared episodes get one primary entry noting
differences, not four near-duplicates.

**The four portraits (book headers):**
- **MARK** — earliest (c. 65–70 CE), urgent and terse; a suffering "Son of Man"; abrupt original ending (16:8).
- **MATTHEW** — Jesus as the new Moses and fulfillment of prophecy; five teaching blocks; written for a Jewish-Christian audience.
- **LUKE** — Jesus as savior of the marginalized (women, the poor, outcasts); paired with Acts; the most "literary."
- **JOHN** — distinct, theological; Jesus as the eternal Word; seven "signs" and seven "I AM" sayings; no parables.
### Infancy and beginnings

> **The prologue — "In the beginning was the Word"** · `John 1:1–18` · *theological hymn* · **[Jn]**
> John opens not with a birth but with the eternal Word (Logos) who "was God" and "became flesh." The high-Christology overture.
> **Related:** echoes Gen 1 and Wisdom (Prov 8); the *theos* in 1:1 is a textual/translation crux (Jehovah's Witnesses render "a god"). **Keywords:** `incarnation` `creation` `theology` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I5 · C5 · P3 · H0`

> **The Annunciation and the Magnificat** · `Luke 1` · *narrative* · **[Lk]**
> Gabriel announces to Mary that she will bear Jesus; she responds with the **Magnificat** ("My soul magnifies the Lord"), a song of reversal. John the Baptist's birth is foretold and celebrated.
> **Related:** echoes Hannah (1 Sam 2); the most-painted NT scene after the Crucifixion; `→quran` Sura 19 (Maryam). **Keywords:** `incarnation` `reversal` `Mary` `→quran`. **Score:** `I5 · C2 · P4 · H0`

> **The Nativity — the birth of Jesus** · `Matt 1–2`; `Luke 2` · *narrative* · **[Mt][Lk]**
> Luke gives the manger, shepherds, and angels; Matthew gives the magi, the star, Herod's massacre of the innocents, and the flight to Egypt. The two infancy accounts differ markedly (genealogies, sequence) — a classic harmonization question.
> **Related:** Christmas; Micah 5:2 (Bethlehem); the most-depicted subject in Western art alongside the Crucifixion. **Keywords:** `incarnation` `Jesus` `Mary` `Bethlehem` `dream-revelation`. **Score:** `I5 · C3 · P5 · H0`

> **John the Baptist; the baptism of Jesus** · `Matt 3`; `Mark 1`; `Luke 3`; `John 1` · *narrative* · **[triple+Jn]**
> John preaches repentance and baptizes Jesus, at which the heavens open, the Spirit descends as a dove, and a voice declares him the beloved Son.
> **Related:** John as the returning Elijah (Mal 4). **Keywords:** `baptism` `repentance` `theophany` `Jesus` `John-the-Baptist`. **Score:** `I4 · C2 · P4 · H0`

> **The temptation in the wilderness** · `Matt 4`; `Luke 4`; `Mark 1` · *narrative* · **[triple, full in Mt/Lk]**
> Jesus fasts forty days and rebuffs three temptations from the devil with scripture.
> **Related:** echoes Israel's forty years; the number forty (Lent). **Keywords:** `testing` `wilderness` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I4 · C2 · P4 · H0`

### Teaching and ministry

> **The Sermon on the Mount / Plain — the Beatitudes** · `Matt 5–7`; cf. `Luke 6` · *teaching discourse* · **[Mt; Lk parallel]**
> Jesus' great ethical teaching: the **Beatitudes** ("blessed are the poor in spirit…"), salt and light, turning the other cheek, the **Lord's Prayer**, "do not worry," the golden rule, and the wise/foolish builders. Luke's shorter "Sermon on the Plain" parallels much of it.
> **Related:** the most influential ethical text in Western history (Tolstoy, Gandhi, King). **Keywords:** `teaching-discourse` `kingdom-of-God` `love` `ethics` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I5 · C3 · P4 · H0`

> **The parables of the kingdom** · `Matt 13`; `Mark 4`; `Luke 8` · *parable* · **[triple + M/L]** — **grouped**
> The sower, the mustard seed, the weeds, the hidden treasure, the pearl — short stories of how God's kingdom grows secretly and surprisingly.
> **Keywords:** `parable` `kingdom-of-God` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H0`

> **The Good Samaritan** · `Luke 10:25–37` · *parable* · **[Lk]**
> Asked "who is my neighbor?", Jesus tells of a despised Samaritan who helps a beaten traveler whom religious figures passed by — redefining neighbor as anyone in need.
> **Related:** "good Samaritan" = a universal idiom and the basis of "Good Samaritan" laws. **Keywords:** `parable` `love` `mercy` `neighbor` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I5 · C2 · P5 · H0`

> **The Prodigal Son** · `Luke 15:11–32` · *parable* · **[Lk]**
> A son squanders his inheritance, returns in shame, and is run to and embraced by his father; the dutiful elder brother resents the welcome. The supreme parable of grace and the harder question of the resentful righteous.
> **Related:** Rembrandt's *Return of the Prodigal Son*; "prodigal" as idiom. **Keywords:** `parable` `repentance` `mercy` `reversal` `sibling-rivalry` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I5 · C2 · P5 · H0`

> **Related:** Matt 16:18 is the central proof-text for the papacy — a defining Catholic/Protestant fault line (the *petros/petra* wordplay). **Keywords:** `discipleship` `messianic-hope` `Peter` `Jesus`. **Score:** `I4 · C5 · P3 · H0`

> **The Transfiguration** · `Matt 17`; `Mark 9`; `Luke 9` · *theophany narrative* · **[triple]**

## THE PAULINE LETTERS
Occasional letters to churches and individuals, broken into argument-sections.
Authorship: seven are undisputed (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians,
1 Thessalonians, Philemon); the others (Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and
the Pastorals — 1–2 Timothy, Titus) are disputed as later/Pauline-school.

> **Romans — the gospel of justification by faith** · `Rom 1–16` · *epistle argument* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> Paul's theological masterwork: universal sin (1–3), justification by faith apart from law (3–5, with Abraham as proof, ch. 4), life in the Spirit (6–8, "nothing can separate us"), the fate of Israel (9–11), and ethical exhortation (12–15).
> **Related:** the engine of the Reformation (Luther on 1:17, "the righteous shall live by faith," from Hab 2:4); Rom 1:26–27 is central to the LGBTQ debate. **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `justification` `faith` `sin` `Paul`. **Score:** `I5 · C5 · P3 · H3`
> *(C5: justification by faith is the defining Reformation fault line; H3: Rom 1 used against LGBTQ people — documented application, contested, framed and rejected.)*

> **1 Corinthians — order, the body, love, resurrection** · `1 Cor 1–16` · *epistle argument* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> Paul addresses a divided church: factions, sexual ethics, idol-meat, worship, spiritual gifts, the "body of Christ," the great **hymn to love** (ch. 13, "though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels"), and the fullest NT teaching on resurrection (ch. 15).
> **Related:** 1 Cor 13 is ubiquitous at weddings; 1 Cor 11 (the Supper). **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `love` `resurrection` `church` `Paul`. **Score:** `I5 · C3 · P3 · H1`

> **2 Corinthians — strength in weakness** · `2 Cor 1–13` · *epistle argument* — **grouped**
> Paul defends his ministry and boasts paradoxically of his weaknesses and his "thorn in the flesh" — "my power is made perfect in weakness."
> **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `suffering` `Paul`. **Score:** `I2 · C2 · P1 · H0`

> **Galatians — freedom from the law** · `Gal 1–6` · *epistle argument* — **grouped**
> Paul's fierce defense of justification by faith against requiring circumcision; "there is neither Jew nor Greek… male nor female… you are all one in Christ" (3:28); the fruit of the Spirit (5).
> **Related:** Gal 3:28 a key text for inclusion/abolition/equality movements. **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `justification` `freedom` `Paul`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P2 · H0`

> **Ephesians; Philippians; Colossians; Philemon — the prison letters** · *epistle* — **grouped, with flagged units**
> **Ephesians** (the church as Christ's body; the "armor of God," 6; the contested "household codes," 5–6); **Philippians** (joy in suffering; the Christ-hymn, 2:5–11, "he emptied himself"); **Colossians** (the cosmic Christ); **Philemon** (a personal plea to receive a runaway slave, Onesimus, "as a brother").
> **Related:** Phil 2 is a key Christology text; Philemon and the household codes figure in the slavery/abolition debate. **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `incarnation` `church` `Paul`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P2 · H3`
> *(H3: the household codes — "slaves, obey your masters" — used to defend slavery; the same canon (Philemon, Gal 3:28) fueled abolition. Documented; framed and rejected.)*

> **1–2 Thessalonians — the return of Christ** · *epistle* — **grouped**
> Comfort about the dead in Christ and teaching on the Lord's coming (the "rapture" proof-text, 1 Thess 4:16–17).
> **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `end-times` `Paul`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P2 · H0`

> **The Pastoral Letters — 1–2 Timothy, Titus** · *epistle* — **grouped, with flagged unit**
> Instructions on church order, leadership, and sound teaching; "all scripture is God-breathed" (2 Tim 3:16); the contested 1 Tim 2:11–15 ("I do not permit a woman to teach").
> **Related:** 1 Tim 2 is central to debates over women's ordination. **Keywords:** `epistle-argument` `church` `leadership`. **Score:** `I3 · C4 · P2 · H3`
> *(H3: 1 Tim 2 used to bar women from teaching/leadership — documented application, contested.)*

## REVELATION — *the apocalypse*
**Genre:** apocalyptic + prophecy + epistle. **Author:** John of Patmos. **Critical:**
c. 95 CE under Roman persecution; richly symbolic. Read variously as
**preterist** (about 1st-c. Rome), **historicist**, **futurist** (end-times to come),
and **idealist** (timeless symbol) — a major interpretive divide.

> **The letters to the seven churches** · `Rev 1–3` · *vision + epistle*
> The glorified Christ dictates tailored messages to seven churches in Asia — praise, rebuke, and promise.
> **Keywords:** `apocalyptic` `vision` `church`. **Score:** `I3 · C3 · P2 · H0`

> **The throne, the scroll, and the seven seals** · `Rev 4–8` · *apocalyptic vision* — **grouped**
> The heavenly throne-room, the slain Lamb worthy to open the scroll, and the **Four Horsemen** unleashed by the seals.
> **Related:** "Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse" = a saturating idiom and art subject. **Keywords:** `apocalyptic` `vision` `judgment` `end-times`. **Score:** `I4 · C4 · P4 · H0`

> **The trumpets, the woman and the dragon, the beast and 666** · `Rev 8–13` · *apocalyptic vision* — **grouped**
> Trumpet plagues, the cosmic battle of the woman, child, and dragon, and the **beast** whose number is **666** — among the most-mined images for end-times speculation.
> **Related:** "666," "the mark of the beast," "Armageddon" (16:16) — pervasive idioms. **Keywords:** `apocalyptic` `vision` `end-times` `judgment`. **Score:** `I4 · C5 · P4 · H2`
> *(H2: "antichrist"/"mark of the beast" rhetoric used to demonize opponents across history; C5: the futurist-vs-preterist divide.)*

> **The fall of Babylon; the millennium and final judgment** · `Rev 14–20` · *apocalyptic vision* — **grouped**
> The harvest of judgment, the fall of "Babylon the great" (Rome), the rider on the white horse, the thousand-year reign (the **millennium** — premil/postmil/amil debate), and the final judgment at the great white throne.
> **Related:** the millennium is a major denominational fault line; "whore of Babylon" in polemic. **Keywords:** `apocalyptic` `judgment` `end-times` `Babylon`. **Score:** `I4 · C5 · P3 · H2`

> **The New Heaven and New Earth; the New Jerusalem** · `Rev 21–22` · *apocalyptic vision*
> God dwells with humanity, "wipes away every tear," and the radiant New Jerusalem descends — no more death, the tree of life restored. The Bible's closing vision, answering Eden.
> **Related:** Gen 2–3 (Eden/tree of life) bookended; Isa 65. **Keywords:** `new-creation` `restoration` `end-times` `hope`. **Score:** `I4 · C3 · P3 · H0`
