# R5 — Ancient Near Eastern & Related Source Texts

The texts from the wider ancient world that the Bible parallels, draws upon, shares
a cultural matrix with, or responds to. Each entry gives the text's **provenance**
(culture, date, language, discovery), its **contents**, the **biblical passage it
parallels** (`→R1`/`→R2`), and the **scholarly view on the relationship** — direct
borrowing, common tradition, polemical response, or coincidence — with disputes
named, not resolved.

**Companion files:** `R0` rubric · `R1` Bible · `R2` Torah · `R3` Quran · `R4` Hadith.

**A framing caution.** "Parallel" does not mean "source." Scholars distinguish
(1) **direct literary dependence**, (2) a **shared cultural/literary tradition** both
texts drew on, (3) **polemic** — the Bible deliberately reworking a known motif to
make an opposite theological point, and (4) **coincidence** of widespread motifs.
Much popular writing (apologetic in both directions) collapses these; this file
keeps them distinct and flags confidence. Primary collections: **ANET** (Pritchard)
and **COS** (Hallo & Younger).

# MESOPOTAMIAN

> **The Epic of Gilgamesh — Tablet XI (the Flood)** · Akkadian; Standard Babylonian version (the flood episode adapted from Atrahasis); the surviving tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, 7th c. BCE; rediscovered and read by George Smith in 1872.
> *Contents:* Gilgamesh, seeking immortality, hears from **Utnapishtim** how the gods sent a flood, instructed him to build a boat, save his family and animals, and how, after the waters, he released a dove, a swallow, and a raven, and offered sacrifice the gods "smelled."
> **Parallels:** Noah's Flood, `→R1` Gen 6–9. Shared elements: divine decision to flood, a chosen survivor, a great boat, animals preserved, **birds sent to test the waters**, a mountain landing, post-flood sacrifice the deity "savors," and a token against future destruction.
> **Scholarly view:** the strongest, least-disputed ANE–Bible parallel. Mainstream scholarship treats the Mesopotamian flood tradition as the **background/source** the biblical account reworked (toward one moral God and a covenant). *(High confidence on the parallel; the mechanism is "dependence on a shared tradition.")* **→R3:** Quran 11, 71 (Nuh).

> **The Atrahasis Epic** · Akkadian; Old Babylonian, c. 1700 BCE.
> *Contents:* humans are created from clay (mixed with a slain god's blood) **to relieve the lesser gods of labor**; when humanity's noise disturbs the gods, they send plague, drought, and finally a **flood**, which Atrahasis survives by building a boat.
> **Parallels:** both the **creation of humans** (`→R1` Gen 2) and the **Flood** (Gen 6–9); the fullest single ANE analogue to Genesis 1–9's overall arc.
> **Scholarly view:** a key witness to the shared flood/creation tradition behind Genesis. *(High confidence.)*

> **Enuma Elish ("When on high")** · Akkadian; Babylonian "creation epic," 7 tablets; recovered at Nineveh (Layard, 1849), first published by George Smith (1876); recited at the Babylonian New Year (*Akitu*).
> *Contents:* from the mingling of primordial fresh water (**Apsu**) and salt-sea chaos (**Tiamat**), the gods emerge; the young god **Marduk** slays Tiamat and splits her body to form sky and earth, then orders the cosmos and creates humanity — a myth that also exalts Babylon and its god.
> **Parallels:** Genesis 1 (`→R1`). Shared: a watery, formless beginning; the Hebrew **tehom** ("the deep," Gen 1:2) is philologically related to **Tiamat**; creation by **separation and ordering**; light/order emerging from chaos.
> **Scholarly view:** divides between older "direct dependence" and the now-common view that Genesis 1 draws on a **shared ANE conceptual world** while **polemically** rejecting its theogony and combat-myth (no birth of gods, no struggle — God simply speaks). *(High confidence on the shared matrix; "borrowing vs. polemic" debated.)* Note the *tehom/Tiamat* link is a real cognate but not proof of literary copying.

> **The Eridu Genesis / Sumerian Flood Story** · Sumerian; fragmentary tablet, c. 1600 BCE.
> *Contents:* the gods create humanity and kingship, antediluvian cities and kings flourish, then a flood is sent, survived by the pious king **Ziusudra**.
> **Parallels:** Gen 1–9's sequence (creation → long-lived ancients → flood); the antediluvian kings parallel the long-lived patriarchs.
> **Scholarly view:** part of the shared flood tradition; the Sumerian layer behind Atrahasis/Gilgamesh.

> **The Sumerian King List** · Sumerian; copies from c. 2000 BCE.
> *Contents:* a list of kings "before the flood" reigning **tens of thousands of years**, then drastically shorter reigns after it.
> **Parallels:** the **long-lived patriarchs** of Gen 5 (Methuselah, 969) and the lifespan collapse after the flood (`→R1`/`→R2`).
> **Scholarly view:** a shared convention of vast antediluvian lifespans shortening after the flood — a **common cultural pattern**, not borrowing. *(Medium confidence.)*

> **The Descent of Inanna / Ishtar to the Underworld** · Sumerian/Akkadian; 2nd millennium BCE.
> *Contents:* the goddess descends through seven gates to the underworld, dies, and is revived — a dying-and-returning deity tied to seasonal fertility (with her consort **Dumuzid/Tammuz**).
> **Parallels:** the mourning for **Tammuz** is named in `→R1` Ezek 8:14; broader "descent/return" motifs are sometimes (contestedly) compared to resurrection imagery.
> **Scholarly view:** a direct biblical mention (Ezekiel) of the cult; the resurrection comparison is **speculative and disputed**.

> **Dumuzid and Enkimdu ("Inanna Prefers the Farmer")** · Sumerian; 2nd millennium BCE.
> *Contents:* the shepherd-god **Dumuzid** and the farmer-god **Enkimdu** compete for the goddess Inanna; the rivalry is resolved **peacefully**, the shepherd favored.
> **Parallels:** Cain and Abel (`→R1` Gen 4) — the **shepherd-vs-farmer** rivalry and the favoring of one vocation.
> **Scholarly view:** a **shared type-scene/motif** (pastoral vs. agrarian tension), proposed by Samuel Noah Kramer. The key difference: the Sumerian tale ends in reconciliation, Genesis in fratricide — so **common motif, not borrowing**. *(Medium confidence; the link is thematic, not textual.)* **→R3:** Quran 5:27–32.

> **The Mesopotamian Law Codes** · Sumerian/Akkadian:
> • **Code of Ur-Nammu** (Sumerian, c. 2100 BCE) — the **earliest known** law code; fines for many injuries.
> • **Laws of Lipit-Ishtar** (Sumerian, c. 1930 BCE) and **Laws of Eshnunna** (Akkadian, c. 1770 BCE).
> • **Code of Hammurabi** (Akkadian, Babylon, c. 1755 BCE) — ~282 laws on a basalt stele (Louvre; found at Susa, 1901), the most famous, featuring *lex talionis* and class-graded penalties.
> **Parallels:** the **Covenant Code** (`→R1`/`→R2` Exod 21–23), Holiness and Deuteronomic codes. Shared **casuistic ("if a man…") form**; **"eye for eye"** (Exod 21:24 // CH 196–201); the **goring ox** (Exod 21:28–36 // CH 250–252).
> **Scholarly view:** a live debate. **David P. Wright** argues the Covenant Code **directly used and revised Hammurabi**; **Bruce Wells** and others argue **shared legal tradition** without literary dependence. Either way, a key **theological contrast**: Hammurabi grades penalties by social class and protects property; biblical law leans toward equal application and valuing human life over property. *(High confidence on the parallel; mechanism debated.)*

> **Ludlul bēl nēmeqi ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom") & the Babylonian Theodicy** · Akkadian; *Ludlul* late 2nd millennium BCE (composed under Nazi-Maruttash); the *Theodicy* c. 1000 BCE.
> *Contents:* a righteous, pious man suffers inexplicably — disease, social ruin, abandonment by his god — and laments the injustice; *Ludlul* ends in restoration; the *Theodicy* is a dialogue debating divine justice.
> **Parallels:** the **Book of Job** (`→R1`) and aspects of certain Psalms — the "righteous sufferer."
> **Scholarly view:** a **shared genre** of the suffering righteous across the ANE; not borrowing. Job's distinctives: a near-blameless sufferer, and a God who actually **appears and answers**. *(High confidence on the genre parallel.)*

# EGYPTIAN

> **The Instruction of Amenemope** · Egyptian; Ramesside period, c. 1300–1075 BCE; best preserved on a papyrus in the British Museum (published 1923).
> *Contents:* a father's "thirty" chapters of practical and ethical wisdom — on contentment, honesty in trade, restraint of anger, and care for the poor.
> **Parallels:** **Proverbs 22:17–24:22** (`→R1`), the "thirty sayings of the wise" (Prov 22:20), with strikingly close maxims (e.g. on not robbing the poor, not moving boundary stones).
> **Scholarly view:** the closest Egyptian–biblical wisdom parallel; **most scholars hold the Hebrew section drew on Amenemope** (or a shared school), making it among the better cases for **direct influence** in the wisdom corpus. *(Medium-high confidence; direction debated by a minority.)*

> **The Tale of Two Brothers** · Egyptian; 19th Dynasty, c. 1200 BCE; Papyrus D'Orbiney (British Museum).
> *Contents:* the virtuous younger brother **Bata** is propositioned by his elder brother's wife; when he refuses, she **falsely accuses him** of assault, nearly costing him his life.
> **Parallels:** **Joseph and Potiphar's wife** (`→R1` Gen 39) — the spurned-seductress / false-accusation motif.
> **Scholarly view:** a **widespread folk motif** (the "Potiphar's wife" or "spurned woman" type, found across cultures); most see a **shared narrative type**, not copying. *(Medium confidence; thematic.)*

> **The Great Hymn to the Aten** · Egyptian; Amarna period, c. 1350 BCE; inscribed in the tomb of the courtier Ay; associated with Pharaoh Akhenaten's sun-disc monotheism.
> *Contents:* a sweeping hymn praising the **Aten** (sun-disc) as sole giver of life — describing day and night, the activity of animals and birds, ships and fish, and the dependence of all creatures on the sun's light.
> **Parallels:** **Psalm 104** (`→R1`) — a sequence of shared images (night when predators roam, day when humans work, God/Aten providing for all creatures).
> **Scholarly view:** the parallels are real and sometimes close, but separated by centuries and language; most scholars favor **indirect transmission or a common ANE hymnic tradition** rather than direct dependence. *(Medium confidence; relationship genuinely unsettled.)*

> **Egyptian creation & afterlife texts** · Egyptian; Memphite Theology (creation by the word/heart of Ptah), Heliopolitan and Hermopolitan cosmogonies; the *Book of the Dead* (judgment of the heart against the feather of Ma'at).
> **Parallels:** **creation by divine speech** (`→R1` Gen 1, "and God said"); weighing-of-deeds imagery resonates with later judgment motifs.
> **Scholarly view:** a **shared ANE conceptual environment** (speech-creation, ordered cosmos); broad, not specific borrowing.

# LEVANTINE / CANAANITE

> **The Ugaritic Texts (Ras Shamra)** · Ugaritic (a West Semitic alphabetic cuneiform); 14th–13th c. BCE; discovered 1929 at Ras Shamra, Syria. The single most important corpus for understanding Israel's Canaanite background.
> *Key texts:* the **Baal Cycle** (KTU 1.1–1.6) — the storm-god **Baal** battles **Yamm** (Sea) and **Mot** (Death), with **El** the high god and **Asherah** the mother-goddess; the **Legend of Keret** and the **Tale of Aqhat** (a righteous king/father loses his children).
> **Parallels:** divine epithets and imagery in the Psalms — **Yahweh as "rider on the clouds"** (Ps 68:4) echoes Baal's epithet; **Psalm 29** (`→R1`) reads almost as a storm-god hymn; **Leviathan / Litan** the sea-dragon (Ps 74; Job 41; Isa 27); the **divine council** and "sons of El" (Ps 82; Deut 32:8–9); the divine names **El** and **Elohim**. Keret/Aqhat's lost children echo Job.
> **Scholarly view:** Israel emerged **within** this West Semitic world and **inherited and repurposed** its poetic vocabulary — often **polemically** (the storm-imagery of Baal redirected to Yahweh; Craigie and others on Ps 29). *(High confidence on shared heritage; specific borrowings debated text by text.)*

> **The Merneptah Stele** · Egyptian; c. 1207 BCE; discovered by Flinders Petrie, 1896 (Cairo).
> *Contents:* a victory inscription of Pharaoh Merneptah that names a defeated people "**Israel**" in Canaan — marked grammatically as a *people*, not a city-state.
> **Parallels:** the **earliest undisputed extra-biblical mention of Israel**; bears on the era of the Judges/settlement (`→R1`).
> **Scholarly view:** a fixed chronological anchor — Israel existed as an entity in Canaan by c. 1207 BCE. *(High confidence.)*

> **The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone)** · Moabite; c. 840 BCE; found 1868 at Dhiban.
> *Contents:* King **Mesha of Moab** boasts of throwing off Israelite control and of his god **Chemosh** — from the Moabite side.
> **Parallels:** the Moabite revolt in `→R1` **2 Kings 3**; it names **Yahweh** and likely the **"House of David"** (a contested reading).
> **Scholarly view:** a rare external, hostile witness corroborating the biblical political world. *(High confidence on the stele; the "House of David" line debated.)*

> **The Tel Dan Stele** · Old Aramaic; c. 840 BCE; discovered 1993–94 at Tel Dan.
> *Contents:* an Aramean king's victory inscription referring to the "king of Israel" and the "**House of David**" (*bytdwd*).
> **Parallels:** the dynasty of **David** (`→R1` 2 Samuel; 1–2 Kings); the conflicts of 2 Kings 8–9.
> **Scholarly view:** widely accepted as the **earliest extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty**, hence to David as a historical dynastic founder. A **minority disputes** the reading (the inscription lacks a word-divider in *bytdwd*). *(High confidence in the mainstream; flagged as contested by minimalists.)*

> **The Amarna Letters** · Akkadian (the diplomatic lingua franca); 14th c. BCE; found at el-Amarna, Egypt.
> *Contents:* diplomatic correspondence between Egypt and Canaanite city-state rulers, mentioning the disruptive **'Apiru / Habiru**.
> **Parallels:** the political landscape of Late Bronze Canaan before Israel's emergence; the **Habiru** are sometimes (contestedly) linked to the term "Hebrew."
> **Scholarly view:** invaluable background to pre-Israelite Canaan; the Habiru–Hebrew equation is **debated and not a simple identity**.

> **Child sacrifice in the Levant — Molech, the *tophet*, and the Punic evidence** · West Semitic / Phoenician-Punic; the biblical polemic 8th–6th c. BCE and later; Punic *tophets* archaeologically c. 8th–2nd c. BCE; classical testimonia (Diodorus, Plutarch).
> *Contents:* a cluster of evidence for offering children (often the firstborn) by fire in the West Semitic world: the Hebrew Bible's repeated condemnation of "passing sons and daughters through the fire" to **Molech** at the **Topheth** in the Valley of Hinnom (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5; 2 Kgs 23:10; Jer 7:31; 19:5; 32:35); the Moabite king **Mesha** sacrificing his firstborn on the city wall to turn a siege (2 Kgs 3:27; cf. the **Mesha Stele** above); and the **Punic *tophet*** precincts at Carthage and across the western Phoenician world — urn-fields of cremated infants and young animals beneath stelae to **Baal Hammon** and **Tanit** — reported as child sacrifice by Greek and Roman writers (Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch, Cleitarchus).
> **Parallels:** the **Binding of Isaac** (`→R1`/`→R2` Gen 22), whose ram-substitution is widely read against this backdrop as a repudiation or transformation of the practice; the **redemption of the firstborn** (Exod 13:11–15; 22:28–29; 34:19–20; Num 18:15), laws that presuppose a firstborn-claim and redirect it; **Jephthah's daughter** (Judg 11:29–40), an unprevented vow-sacrifice; **Ezek 20:25–26** and **Mic 6:7** ("shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?"); and kings **Ahaz** and **Manasseh**, who "passed their sons through the fire" (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6). The Valley of Hinnom (*Ge-Hinnom*) later becomes **Gehenna**, a word for hell.
> **Scholarly view:** *that* child sacrifice was practiced in the Canaanite/Phoenician-Punic world and *that* the Bible vehemently condemns it is **well-attested** — the polemic is dense and the Punic urn-fields are real. Two things are genuinely **contested**: (1) whether the Punic *tophets* are sacrificial precincts or ordinary infant cemeteries — the majority now read them as (at least partly) sacrificial (**Stager & Wolff**; **Xella et al.**, 2013), against a minority "natural-death burial" view; and (2) how far **Israelites themselves** practiced it, and whether YHWH was once thought to claim the firstborn — **Jon D. Levenson** (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993) and **Francesca Stavrakopoulou** argue the firstborn-claim was real and deeply embedded, later redeemed and repudiated, while others read the Bible as condemning a wholly foreign rite. Note too the philological debate over **"Molech"**: **Otto Eissfeldt** (*Molk als Opferbegriff*, 1935) argued *mlk* named a *type of offering* (cf. Punic *mlk*), later misvocalized as a god's name. *(High confidence that the practice and the polemic existed; the Israelite extent — and whether the Aqedah transforms the rite or merely condemns it — are debated.)* **→R3:** Quran 37 (the near-sacrifice; the son generally Ishmael; the basis of *Eid al-Adha*).

# PERSIAN / ZOROASTRIAN (a bridging influence)

> **Zoroastrian thought** · Old Iranian (Avestan); the Gathas attributed to Zarathustra (dating debated, c. 1500–1000 BCE), but the key textual witnesses are much later, which complicates claims of influence.
> *Contents:* a **cosmic dualism** of truth/light (Ahura Mazda) vs. the lie/darkness (Angra Mainyu); judgment of the soul; bodily **resurrection** and a final renovation of the world; ranks of spiritual beings.
> **Parallels:** developments visible in **post-exilic and Second Temple Judaism** (`→R1` Daniel; and the intertestamental texts below): **named angels** (Michael, Gabriel), a more defined **demonology**, **light/darkness dualism** (cf. the Dead Sea "War Scroll"), **bodily resurrection** (Dan 12), and the evolution of **ha-satan** from a heavenly prosecutor (Job 1–2) toward an adversarial power.
> **Scholarly view:** the **Babylonian exile and Persian period** (from 539 BCE) are the proposed contact zone. Some scholars affirm substantial Zoroastrian **influence**; others judge it **unproven**, chiefly because the Zoroastrian texts are attested late, making the direction and dating of influence uncertain. *(Genuinely contested — present both positions; do not assert.)*

# SECOND TEMPLE / INTERTESTAMENTAL (a bridging corpus)

Jewish texts of c. 300 BCE–100 CE that connect the Hebrew Bible to the New Testament
(`→R1`), the Quran (`→R3`), and later tradition. Not in the Protestant canon (some
are in Catholic/Orthodox canons; others are pseudepigrapha), but essential context.

> **1 Enoch** · Ge'ez (full text), from Aramaic/Hebrew originals; 3rd c. BCE onward; fragments at Qumran.
> *Contents:* the **Watchers** (fallen angels who corrupt humanity — expanding Gen 6:1–4), heavenly journeys, and the "Son of Man."
> **Parallels:** expands `→R1` Gen 6; **quoted in Jude 14–15**; shapes NT angelology and apocalyptic; resonates with the *Mi'raj* ascent tradition (`→R4`).

> **Jubilees** · 2nd c. BCE; a "rewritten Bible" retelling Genesis–Exodus on a solar-calendar, 49-year ("jubilee") scheme; popular at Qumran.
> **Parallels:** a witness to how Second Temple Jews **retold and harmonized** Genesis — methodologically akin to later *midrash* (`→R2`) and *qisas al-anbiya* (`→R4`).

> **The Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran)** · Hebrew/Aramaic/Greek; c. 250 BCE–68 CE; discovered 1947 onward.
> *Contents:* the **oldest biblical manuscripts** (a millennium older than the medieval Masoretic Text), plus sectarian works (the Community Rule, the War Scroll's light-vs-darkness dualism).
> **Parallels:** textual witness to the OT (`→R1`/`→R2`) showing both stability and variety in the text; the dualism bears on the Persian-influence question above.

> **The Septuagint (LXX)** · Koine Greek; 3rd–2nd c. BCE, Alexandria.
> *Contents:* the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures (plus the deuterocanonical books).
> **Parallels:** the form of the OT **most quoted in the New Testament** (`→R1`); differs from the Masoretic Text in many readings (famously Isa 7:14 "virgin"/"young woman"), making it a key witness in textual criticism and in Jewish–Christian interpretive debates.

## Apparatus (R5)
**Coverage:** the principal Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Levantine/Canaanite, Persian,
and Second Temple texts with provenance, contents, biblical parallel, and the
relationship debate. Confidence labels and the four mechanisms (dependence /
shared tradition / polemic / coincidence) are applied per entry.
**Sourcing note:** primary translations in **ANET** (Pritchard) and **COS** (Hallo &
Younger); this file prefers academic framing over apologetic sources in either
direction, and flags where a relationship is genuinely unsettled (Aten/Ps 104;
Zoroastrian influence; the Tel Dan reading; Covenant Code dependence; the Punic
*tophet* interpretation and the Israelite extent of child sacrifice).
**Cross-corpus:** `→R1` Bible, `→R2` Torah, `→R3` Quran, `→R4` Hadith.
